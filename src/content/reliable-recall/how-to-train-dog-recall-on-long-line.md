A long line is most useful when it prevents a mistake without becoming the reason the dog comes back.

That distinction matters. If every outdoor recall ends with the dog being pulled toward you, the line is doing the behavior. The training goal is different: the dog hears the cue, chooses to turn, travels all the way back, enters handling reach, and can be safely secured or released.

## Start with equipment you can actually handle

For running and recall-proofing work, attach the line to a sturdy, well-fitted harness rather than relying on a neck collar for a dog that may accelerate to the end.

Before a session, check:

- harness fit;
- clip and stitching;
- cuts, knots, or damage in the line;
- whether the route has enough room to use the chosen length;
- whether people, dogs, bicycles, trees, benches, or other obstacles could become entanglement hazards;
- whether traffic, livestock, wildlife, water, cliffs, or local rules make the planned exercise inappropriate.

A longer line is not automatically better. A 15-foot line may be easier on a wooded path; 20 to 30 feet can create useful choice in a clear open space. Use the length you can manage fluently.

Never wrap the line around your hand, wrist, or fingers.

## Practice the line before practicing recall

The first useful session may contain no deliberate recalls.

In a quiet place, practice paying line out as the dog moves away and gathering it as the dog comes closer. Aim for a soft curve rather than continuous tension. Shorten early before a narrow section, another walker, a dog, or an obstacle.

If the equipment consumes all your attention, the recall setup is too complicated. Line handling should become boring enough that you can watch the dog and the environment.

## Begin closer than the line allows

Owning a 30-foot line does not mean the first recall should happen at 30 feet.

Start at a distance where the dog is already likely to succeed. Prepare the reward before you need it, but keep it out of sight until the dog commits to returning.

When safe:

1. Wait for slack in the line.
2. Give the recall cue once.
3. Move away if that helps the dog choose you.
4. Mark the turn or committed return if you use a marker.
5. Reward close to your body.
6. Briefly touch the harness or clip the leash when useful.
7. Release again when the situation allows.

The dog should learn that your body is the destination, not merely a point to run past.

## Keep the line from becoming a towing cable

If the dog does not respond, avoid a long hand-over-hand pull.

Maintain safety, shorten the distance, move away from the distraction if possible, and reset. Your next repetition should be easier.

That might mean:

- calling from closer range;
- moving farther from another dog;
- leaving the fresh scent patch;
- returning to a familiar field;
- shortening the session;
- waiting until arousal drops.

Two failed or assisted recalls in the same setup are enough information to change the picture.

## Build recall into an ordinary walk

A proofing walk does not need to become a constant drill.

Run a few planned repetitions while the dog is already exploring on the line. Many successful recalls can end with reward, a brief handling touch, and a release back to sniffing.

That helps prevent a common problem: the dog learns that “come” always means the interesting part of the walk is finished.

For the scent-specific version of this pattern, see [How to Recall a Dog Away From Sniffing](/reliable-recall/dog-recall-when-sniffing/).

## Add distance in rungs

Once the dog responds cleanly at short range, create a distance ladder that fits your space—for example 6, 10, 15, 20, and 30 feet.

Do not simply march upward every repetition. Mix easier distances back in. If the turn slows, the dog detours, or the final approach becomes incomplete, drop a rung and restore speed and clarity.

Progress is better defined by several first-cue returns across more than one session than by one dramatic maximum-distance success.

## Know what the line cannot make safe

A long line is not permission to gamble near traffic, livestock, cliffs, unsafe water, protected wildlife, or anywhere the consequences of failure are serious. It can also be unsuitable in crowded spaces where it could trip or entangle other people or dogs.

Sometimes a standard leash, fenced area, or a different location is the better training choice.

The aim is not to reach the end of the longest line. It is to build a voluntary return while management keeps the cost of a mistake low.

When the dog has repeated evidence in the relevant environment and distraction class, use the [Off-Leash Readiness Check](/reliable-recall/dog-off-leash-readiness-check/) before treating the line as optional.
