A landscape estimate becomes easier to review when each question is answered in the right order.

The customer may ask a simple question — **What will this job cost?** — but a reliable answer depends on several different kinds of information.

A useful sequence is:

1. **Scope**
2. **Quantify**
3. **Time**
4. **Cost**
5. **Price**
6. **Check & Learn**

The point is not to make estimating complicated. It is to stop different decisions from being hidden inside one number.

## Pass 1: Scope — what exactly are you promising?

Before calculating square feet or material quantities, describe the work.

For a bed renovation, that can mean identifying:

- what existing plants stay or go;
- whether old surface material is removed;
- whether soil amendment is included;
- who selects plant sizes and species;
- whether irrigation changes are included;
- what happens to excavated or removed material;
- whether haul-away is included;
- what the completion standard looks like.

Also record conditions that can affect production:

- access and staging;
- slopes;
- visible soil and surface conditions;
- roots and stumps;
- nearby walls, paving, fences, or structures;
- utility-locating requirements;
- traffic or parking restrictions.

Unknowns should remain visible as unknowns. Do not silently convert uncertainty into a fixed-price assumption.

A useful scope sheet should be clear enough that another competent person in the company can understand what you intend to price without immediately needing a list of basic clarifications.

## Pass 2: Quantify — how much physical work is there?

Measure the job in the units that describe the work:

- square feet;
- linear feet;
- cubic yards;
- plant counts;
- loads;
- trips;
- equipment hours;
- other measurable units.

Keep the measurement and the purchasing decision separate.

A bed may measure to a theoretical mulch volume, but the supplier may sell in half-yard increments. A paver area may require project-specific cutting overage. Sod waste on a simple rectangle is not the same as sod waste around curves and trees.

The estimator's job is not merely to multiply a measurement by a generic waste percentage. Ask what physical mechanism could make the amount purchased differ from the amount theoretically required.

For a deeper material workflow, see [measured quantity versus purchase quantity](/landscape-contractor-estimating/landscape-material-purchase-quantity/).

## Pass 3: Time — how many labor-hours will the work consume?

"This looks like a two-day job" is not yet a labor estimate.

A three-person crew working two eight-hour days consumes 48 labor-hours, not 16.

Break the job into operations such as:

- mobilize and protect;
- remove or demolish;
- excavate;
- haul;
- grade or prepare;
- install;
- cut or finish;
- clean up;
- demobilize.

Then estimate the labor-hours for each operation.

Production information is more useful when it comes from comparable completed company work. If past jobs show how many labor-hours a defined operation normally uses under defined conditions, that evidence can support the next assumption.

Crew productivity is not always linear. A narrow gate, one saw, one machine, one skilled installer, or one wheelbarrow route can become the bottleneck.

See [how to estimate landscape labor-hours](/landscape-contractor-estimating/estimate-landscape-labor-hours/) for a fuller method.

## Pass 4: Cost — what will this job consume?

Build the job cost deliberately.

Common categories include:

- direct labor;
- direct materials;
- equipment;
- subcontractors;
- delivery;
- disposal;
- project-specific permits or fees where applicable;
- travel or procurement under the company's chosen method;
- other job-specific costs.

Do not treat owned equipment as free simply because no rental invoice arrives.

Do not forget the smaller operations around the visible installation. Two employees spending 90 minutes collecting material use three labor-hours before vehicle cost is considered. Disposal may include both a tipping charge and the time, truck, trailer, and labor required to make the trip.

Every real cost needs one deliberate home. It should not disappear, and it should not be recovered twice.

## Pass 5: Price — how will cost become a selling price?

Cost and price are different questions.

Once the estimate includes the costs the company's model is designed to recover, apply the defined pricing architecture.

That may involve:

- a fully loaded cost plus a target operating margin;
- direct cost plus a gross-margin structure intended to recover overhead and profit;
- category-specific recovery factors.

There is no universal percentage in this workflow.

The business needs to understand:

- what is already inside the cost base;
- how overhead is recovered;
- whether the percentage is markup or margin;
- what return the pricing model is intended to produce.

If you cannot explain what a percentage is doing, do not automate it yet.

The [markup versus margin guide](/landscape-contractor-estimating/landscape-markup-vs-margin/) shows the arithmetic distinction.

## Pass 6: Check & Learn — review before and after

Before the quote leaves the business, challenge the estimate.

Check:

- scope;
- units and quantity conversions;
- purchase quantities;
- labor-hours;
- current supplier costs;
- delivery;
- equipment;
- disposal;
- overhead recovery;
- markup or margin arithmetic;
- major uncertainties.

Then preserve the estimate you sold.

After the work, compare estimate with actual results:

- quantities;
- purchases;
- labor-hours;
- equipment;
- subcontractors;
- disposal;
- major scope changes.

Do not overwrite the original estimate with actual data. You need both versions if you want to learn.

## Why the sequence matters

A low final price can come from several different causes.

Perhaps the material quantity is wrong.

Perhaps an operation is missing.

Perhaps labor-hours are too optimistic.

Perhaps owned equipment was treated as free.

Perhaps overhead was omitted.

Perhaps markup was mistaken for margin.

Those are different problems. A single blanket percentage does not tell you which one occurred.

The Six-Pass Estimate keeps the chain visible enough to inspect:

**Scope → Quantify → Time → Cost → Price → Check & Learn**

That makes the final price an output of the estimating process rather than the first guess.
