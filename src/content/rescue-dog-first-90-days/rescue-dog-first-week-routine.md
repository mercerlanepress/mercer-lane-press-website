After the first day, the goal is not to keep the dog isolated for a fixed number of days.

It is to reduce unnecessary demands while beginning the ordinary routines that make the home understandable.

A useful first week is built around **anchors**, not a rigid clock.

## Use a few daily anchors

A practical day may include:

- morning toilet soon after waking;
- breakfast within a familiar window;
- a rest period after morning activity;
- a midday toilet trip or manageable walk;
- quiet time while the household works;
- an evening meal and appropriate activity;
- a last toilet opportunity before bed.

Small variation is fine.

The point is not to create a dog that depends on breakfast arriving at an exact minute. The point is to make the day's broad shape predictable.

## Keep the world small enough to learn

In the first days, repeated familiar places are useful.

Use:

- the same toilet area;
- similar meal locations;
- a small set of rooms;
- short familiar walking routes;
- the same basic door procedure;
- the same sleeping setup.

The dog is learning patterns: which door leads outside, where water appears, how household movement works, where rest is protected, and what the lead predicts.

Every boring repetition gives information.

## Protect sleep before adding more activity

A new dog can look restless and still be tired.

Novelty, unfamiliar sounds, people, surfaces, and routines can keep the dog alert even while lying down.

Make genuine rest easier by:

- placing the bed away from heavy foot traffic;
- blocking a busy window if movement keeps the dog scanning;
- teaching children and adults that the rest area is off-limits;
- using a gate or calm zone if following every movement prevents sleep;
- reducing unnecessary noise and interaction.

Do not solve every inability to settle by adding a longer walk.

## Rebuild the toilet pattern even with an adult dog

A previously house-trained dog can have accidents after rehoming.

For the first week, give frequent opportunities after:

- waking;
- meals;
- active play;
- long rest periods;
- before bed;
- any individual pre-toilet signal you begin to recognize.

Reward outside toileting promptly.

If accidents occur, clean thoroughly and adjust the opportunity schedule rather than punishing after the fact.

Pain, blood, straining, unusually frequent toileting, marked thirst, diarrhea, vomiting, or a sudden change should be discussed with a veterinarian.

## Keep food changes slow

Unless medical guidance says otherwise, avoid abrupt food changes during an already demanding transition.

Food can also support early training, but the dog does not need to earn every calorie.

Use part of the normal ration for easy behaviors such as:

- checking in;
- settling;
- moving calmly through a doorway;
- toileting outside;
- tolerating harness handling.

If the dog guards food or high-value items, do not repeatedly reach into the bowl or take items to prove ownership. Manage distance and seek qualified guidance when guarding creates risk.

## Let interaction happen without making it a full-time task

Spend time in the same room without constantly engaging the dog.

Read, work, make food, or watch television while allowing voluntary approach.

Reward calm check-ins if that helps, but do not turn every moment into a cue or social demand.

A dog needs time to discover that people can be present without always touching, training, calling, or moving toward them.

## Start a three-day baseline log

Write facts rather than labels.

Useful headings include:

- food;
- water;
- toileting;
- sleep;
- walks;
- people;
- sounds;
- other animals;
- brief alone moments;
- health observations.

“Paced for fifteen minutes after a delivery van” is more useful than “nervous dog.”

The log helps you see change and decide what to keep easy.

## Reward the life you want without drilling obedience

Training can begin immediately if it is low pressure.

Useful early behaviors to reinforce include:

- lying on the bed or mat;
- checking in on a walk;
- moving through a doorway calmly;
- toileting outside;
- allowing brief harness handling;
- noticing a mild distraction and turning away;
- settling after activity;
- choosing distance instead of escalation.

Short repetitions are enough.

## Use the end of day three as a review, not a deadline

If the dog is still struggling to eat, sleep, toilet, or move around the home comfortably, keep the setup simple.

If ordinary home life is mostly comfortable, add **one small thing**:

- open one additional room;
- extend a familiar walk slightly;
- add a short training game;
- allow one calm household member slightly more interaction.

Changing one important variable makes the dog's response easier to interpret.

## Days four to seven: build routine before social plans

The end of the first week often creates pressure to “get back to normal.”

That can lead to several new challenges at once: visitors, longer walks, family gatherings, dog meetings, grooming, busy public places, or a training class.

Instead, ask:

- what helps this dog settle?
- what reliably raises tension?
- what route is currently manageable?
- which household rules are actually being followed?
- are sleep, eating, drinking, toileting, and recovery becoming more predictable?
- what one new challenge, if any, should be added next?

Choose no more than a few early training priorities.

A common set is:

- name/check-in;
- mat;
- comfortable harness and lead handling.

## The first-week checkpoint

Move forward when ordinary home life is mostly comfortable or only mildly difficult and the household can manage safety reliably.

Stay at the current level—or get help—when the dog is repeatedly panicking, cannot recover from ordinary events, has significant medical signs, or presents a risk the household cannot manage.
