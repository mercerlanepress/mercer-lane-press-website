A volunteer coach can spend longer planning practice than running it.

One search for a passing drill becomes another search for a warm-up, then a shooting game, then a formation video.

The problem is not a lack of activities.

It is the lack of a repeatable way to decide **what this team needs next**.

A useful practice plan can begin with five questions.

## 1. What did I notice last time?

Choose one thing.

Examples:

- players crowd around the ball;
- nobody looks before receiving;
- players stop after losing possession;
- passing becomes rushed;
- defenders dive toward the ball;
- players do not move into useful space;
- finishing becomes hurried near goal.

Do not list every mistake from the previous match.

The aim is to create one manageable learning problem.

A short post-match note can help. Write:

**KEEP — CHANGE — TRY**

The TRY item is often the starting point for the next practice.

## 2. What do I want players to do more often?

Turn the problem into an action you can actually see.

Instead of:

**Improve teamwork**

use:

**When our team has the ball, move away from the crowd and give the ball carrier another option.**

Instead of:

**Improve defending**

use:

**Get between the opponent and goal before trying to win the ball.**

Instead of:

**Pass better**

use:

**Look before receiving and pass toward a teammate in useful space.**

If you cannot see the behavior, it will be difficult to coach it clearly.

## 3. What game or activity creates that problem?

Now choose the activity.

This order matters.

Starting with a favorite drill and only later deciding what it teaches often creates unnecessary complexity.

If the problem is crowding, a slightly wider small-sided game may create more useful space.

If the problem is looking before receiving, give players targets, teammates or directions they need to notice.

If the problem is transition after losing possession, use a game in which possession changes frequently.

The activity does not need many rules.

Ask:

**Does the player have something to see, decide or respond to?**

If yes, you are probably keeping the practice connected to the game.

## 4. What will I say?

Choose one or two cues.

Examples:

- "Can you find space away from the crowd?"
- "Look before the ball arrives."
- "Can we win it back quickly?"
- "Give the player with the ball another option."

Short cues are easier for children to process and easier for the coach to remember.

They also leave room for the players to make decisions.

A useful test is:

**Can this player do something with my comment on the next repetition?**

If not, shorten it.

## 5. What will I do if the numbers change?

Attendance changes.

Plan for it before practice.

Write one contingency:

- fewer than eight: use 3v3 instead of 4v4;
- odd number: use a neutral player;
- more than twelve: create two copies of the same game;
- no assistant: simplify the organization and keep the group in one manageable area.

Do not design a session that only works if exactly ten players arrive.

The coaching objective can stay the same while the numbers change.

## Put it on one small practice card

Your plan can fit into eight lines:

**TODAY'S FOCUS:**  
**ARRIVAL PLAY:**  
**FIRST GAME:**  
**PRACTICE ACTIVITY:**  
**FINAL GAME:**  
**COACHING CUE 1:**  
**COACHING CUE 2:**  
**IF NUMBERS CHANGE:**  

That is enough to walk onto the field with a plan.

## Use Play → Practice → Play Again

A simple structure is:

### Play

Get players into soccer-like activity quickly.

Let the game show you the problem.

### Practice

Use a focused activity that gives players repeated opportunities to work on the problem.

### Play again

Return to the game.

See whether anything changes.

This is a planning shorthand, not a rule that replaces your club or association curriculum.

If your organization provides an age-group program, use it as the primary framework and adapt your session accordingly.

## Example: everyone crowds the ball

Your card might look like this:

**Focus:** create useful space when our team has possession.

**Arrival:** 3v3 game.

**First game:** watch where players stand when a teammate has the ball.

**Practice activity:** 3v3 or 4v4 in a wider area.

**Cue 1:** "Can you give your teammate somewhere to pass?"

**Cue 2:** "Can someone find space away from the ball?"

**Final game:** normal small-sided soccer.

**Numbers contingency:** use a neutral player for an odd number; split into two smaller games if attendance is high.

That is a complete session concept.

## Reuse what works

A successful activity does not need to disappear because you already used it.

Reuse it.

Change one variable:

- the field size;
- team numbers;
- scoring direction;
- target;
- pressure;
- challenge;
- coaching focus.

Familiar activities reduce explanation time.

That means more playing time.

## When the plan is wrong

Sometimes the activity fails immediately.

Players do not understand it. There is too much waiting. The space is wrong. The difficulty is wrong.

Use a quick rescue:

1. stop;
2. identify the actual problem;
3. change one thing;
4. restart.

If necessary, abandon the activity and play a small-sided game.

Changing a bad plan is part of coaching.
