A surprise encounter is a management event, not a test of whether your training “works.”

Another dog can appear around a blind corner. A runner can pass from behind. Elevator doors can open onto a dog. A cyclist can approach quickly. A loose dog can begin closing distance. In these moments, the useful decision is usually much simpler than people expect.

## Use four steps: move, block, simplify, recover

### 1. Move

Turn, cross the road if it is safe, arc away, or retreat to the last wider area.

Do not wait for barking or lunging to prove that the trigger is too close. The five seconds before the reaction are often the most valuable time you have.

If you have practiced a turn-away cue in easy places, use it as motion: cue, turn, move, and reinforce after you have created space. Do not stop in the middle of an exit to ask for a stationary sit while the trigger continues closing distance.

### 2. Block the view

Cars, hedges, walls, trees, dumpsters, and building corners can reduce visual pressure long enough for you to leave.

Learn the usable barriers on your regular routes before you need them. A barrier does not solve the underlying behavior; it simply helps you restore a workable situation.

### 3. Keep it simple

Do not try to “salvage a rep” while a surprise trigger moves closer.

If the dog can eat while moving, several small treats after you have created space may help. If the dog cannot eat, keep moving to safety and reward later. Repeatedly offering food while the dog remains locked on is not the same as restoring flexibility.

If another owner is approaching, use a short script rather than a long explanation:

**“Please give us space; we're training.”**

For an off-leash dog, call clearly for the owner to recall the dog, move away, and use barriers where available. Keep hands away from the space between dogs.

### 4. Recover

After a difficult exit, make the rest of the walk easier. Choose a quieter street, allow low-pressure sniffing, or go home.

Do not seek another trigger just to “end on a good one.” The useful outcome is that the dog gets back to normal walking behavior without being pushed into another avoidable confrontation.

## Tight spaces need staging, not bravery

Hallways, gates, stairwells, elevators, narrow paths, and crowded sidewalks remove your best tool: distance.

Control entry and timing whenever possible:

- listen before opening an apartment door;
- stand back from elevator doors until you know who is inside;
- pause before a blind gate or alley exit;
- identify the nearest wider retreat point before entering a narrow path;
- use quieter access times if a bottleneck is repeatedly difficult.

If another dog is already occupying the tight space, waiting or retreating can be the better training decision.

## What to review later

Once everyone is safe, ask only what will improve the next walk:

- What was the first warning?
- How much distance did you have?
- Was there a bottleneck?
- Which exit worked?
- How long did recovery take?
- What route change would give more warning next time?
