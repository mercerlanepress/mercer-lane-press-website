A scheduled maintenance system looks forward from known requirements. A defect workflow handles the new information that appears between scheduled visits: a warning light, unusual noise, tire damage, a change in braking feel, a fluid leak, a door problem, or another observation.

The administrative goal is not to turn drivers or office staff into technicians. It is to make sure a report becomes a controlled next action instead of disappearing into a message thread.

## Record the observation before the diagnosis

Capture what the reporter actually saw, heard, felt, or read. Useful fields include:

- Defect ID and Unit ID;
- date and time;
- mileage;
- reporter and vehicle location;
- the observation in the reporter's words;
- when the issue occurs;
- exact dashboard wording where safely obtainable;
- photo or reference where useful;
- current vehicle status;
- initial next action;
- owner and target date.

Do not silently turn “vibration at a certain speed” into a specific component failure. A qualified provider may later diagnose a different cause. Keeping observation and diagnosis separate preserves trustworthy history.

## Give every open item three controls

At minimum, every open defect or repair action needs:

1. **Owner** - the person responsible for moving it.
2. **Next action** - contact provider, book diagnosis, obtain a quote, await parts, seek approval, or another defined step.
3. **Target or review date** - the point at which the action should happen or be chased.

If one of those fields is missing, the item is parked rather than managed.

A small status model can move records through stages such as NEW, REVIEW REQUIRED, QUOTE / DIAGNOSIS REQUIRED, APPROVAL REQUIRED, BOOKED, IN PROGRESS, WAITING, COMPLETE, and CLOSED. Use only the states your team can maintain consistently.

## Keep a clear escalation boundary

The system should make it easy to mark a vehicle held or out of service and show who made that operational decision, why, what professional or managerial action comes next, and what evidence supports return to service.

The spreadsheet itself should not decide whether an unfamiliar warning or symptom is safe. Define your organization's escalation route from the current rules, company policy, manufacturer guidance, and qualified advice that apply to the operation.

## Separate inspection, decision, authorization, and work

A useful chain is:

**Observation → Review / inspection → Decision → Authorized work → Completion → History update**

That prevents two common misunderstandings: that a reported problem is already approved for repair, or that somebody looking at the vehicle means the issue is closed.

For material work, a repair authorization record can capture:

- Authorization ID and Unit ID;
- linked defect or service item;
- vendor;
- requested scope;
- quote or estimate reference;
- expected amount and internal approval limit;
- approver and date;
- appointment or drop-off;
- approved scope changes;
- final invoice;
- recommendations or work not completed.

Set internal approval thresholds before an urgent phone call forces an improvised decision. These are business controls, not universal monetary recommendations.

## Compare quote scope, not just headline price

Two quotes may include different diagnosis, parts, labor, fees, warranty terms, timing, and exclusions. Record enough information to see those differences and ask better commercial questions.

If the provider finds additional work, use a documented change-approval route rather than letting the final invoice become the first record of the changed scope.

## Do not lose declined or deferred recommendations

A service invoice may contain work that was recommended but not completed. If it matters, create a linked open action with a clear status: plan next visit, seek another opinion, monitor per qualified advice, or another documented disposition.

Do not rely on somebody remembering to reopen the PDF later.

## Close the repair administratively

The keys returning is not the end of the record. Close the relevant event only when the useful information is captured:

- return-to-service status;
- completion date and mileage;
- provider;
- work summary;
- invoice or service report;
- cost under your defined categories;
- linked defect/action disposition;
- follow-up recommendations;
- the recurring rule actually satisfied by the work;
- the correct next-due reset;
- downtime closure where applicable.

If the vehicle is available but paperwork is missing, a state such as **COMPLETE - AWAITING CLOSURE** can keep the administrative gap visible.

## Test the workflow with messy scenarios

Before rollout, test the process with examples such as a duplicate report, an intermittent issue, a provider recommendation found during planned service, “no fault found,” a vehicle returning before the invoice, and an after-hours report.

The test is simple: can each case retain what was observed, show who owns the next action, preserve the decision trail, and reach a documented disposition?
