The useful question on a reactive-dog walk is not “How close can we get?” It is “At what distance can my dog notice the trigger and still remain flexible?”

That workable distance will change with context. A dog moving away may be easier than one approaching head-on. One dog across an open road may be manageable while two dogs at a narrow gate are not. A runner, bicycle, loud vehicle, or surprise from behind a parked car may need a different amount of space.

## Look for your dog's yellow zone

Compare the dog with its own normal walking baseline rather than relying on a universal checklist.

Early changes can include:

- the mouth closing;
- sudden stillness;
- harder staring;
- slowing or speeding up;
- weight shifting forward or back;
- tail or ear position changing;
- panting suddenly stopping;
- scanning;
- refusing food;
- pulling toward or away from the trigger.

Think in three practical zones.

**Green:** movement is loose, normal sniffing or eating continues, and check-ins are easy.

**Yellow:** the dog has clearly noticed the trigger and attention is narrowing, but you still have useful choices. This is the point to create space, arc away, turn, or use a barrier before the situation becomes harder.

**Red:** barking, lunging, freezing, frantic scanning, inability to turn away, or unsafe control means the job is exit and recovery rather than teaching.

## Food is one clue, not the whole test

A dog who normally loves food and suddenly cannot take or swallow a familiar treat may be too aroused for the planned exercise. But the reverse is also important: some dogs will eat while remaining rigid and intensely fixed on a trigger.

Combine food response with movement, posture, breathing, and the ability to disengage.

If the dog rapidly eats a treat and immediately snaps back into a hard stare, increase distance. Do not use eating alone as proof that the setup is easy enough.

## Start farther away than you think you need

Let the dog notice the trigger. Reinforce a return of attention or movement with you. The dog does not need to pretend the trigger is absent; the useful pattern is noticing without becoming locked on.

If the dog cannot turn back easily, make the setup easier by increasing distance. If the dog can notice and return several times with soft movement, that is useful information.

Do not automatically reduce distance during the same session. Repeatable quality matters more than today's closest pass.

A worked example in *The Reactive Dog Walking Playbook* uses about **100–115 ft (30–35 m)** as workable for one dog while about **65 ft (20 m)** with two dogs approaching head-on is too difficult. Those figures are examples, not targets for another dog. Your own log should establish your own ranges.

## Progress by changing one variable at a time

You can make a setup harder by changing distance, location, duration, movement, trigger type, or number of triggers. Avoid changing several at once.

A practical progression might look like this:

1. Repeat a generous distance until results are broadly consistent.
2. Reduce distance slightly **or** use a slightly different location.
3. Repeat that new level across more than one session.
4. If fixation, reactions, or recovery time increase, step back.

A “good” repetition also includes what happens afterward. Faster recovery, more normal sniffing, easier exits, and more usable routes are meaningful progress even if occasional reactions still happen.

When distance disappears unexpectedly, switch from training to management. The guide on [what to do when a trigger appears too close](/reactive-dog-walking/what-to-do-trigger-too-close-reactive-dog/) covers that decision.

> **Safety note:** Do not deliberately expose a dog to a trigger at a level that causes panic or loss of control. If biting, attempted biting, redirected aggression, escape, or safe-control concerns are present, stop self-directed exposure work and obtain individualized help.
