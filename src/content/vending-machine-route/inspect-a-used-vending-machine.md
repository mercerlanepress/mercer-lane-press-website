Used vending equipment can reduce initial capital, but condition must be investigated rather than assumed.

The objective of an inspection is not to prove that nothing will ever break. It is to identify avoidable risk before money changes hands and to understand what the machine will cost to place into reliable service.

If electrical, refrigeration, or other technical systems are outside your competence, use appropriately qualified help.

## Before you travel

Ask the seller for:

- manufacturer and exact model;
- serial number;
- clear photographs;
- dimensions;
- known faults;
- recent repairs;
- keys;
- current payment configuration;
- approximate operating history.

Research the exact model before viewing it. Check whether parts, manuals, controller information, and practical support are reasonably available.

Do not assume that a familiar cabinet means the internal control system or payment compatibility is the same as another machine.

## Inspect the cabinet and interior

Look for structural and security problems before cosmetic defects.

Check for:

- corrosion;
- water damage;
- broken glass;
- damaged doors;
- forced-entry marks;
- failed seals;
- missing panels;
- damaged shelves;
- poor or improvised wiring;
- burnt connectors;
- missing components;
- signs of pests.

Do not energize equipment that appears unsafe.

A machine that looks tired can still be serviceable. A machine with hidden structural, electrical, refrigeration, or support problems can become expensive even when the purchase price is low.

## Test the display, controls, and vend system

Power the machine safely and test the functions you expect to rely on.

Check the display, keypad, selection buttons, price programming, and error states. Run more than one test vend using representative products where practical.

Inspect motors, spirals or channels, shelves, delivery mechanisms, and any selections that hang or fail.

One successful vend does not demonstrate that every selection works.

## Verify cash and cashless operation

If cash will remain enabled, test the bill validator, coin mechanism, and change operation.

For cashless systems, do not accept a broad statement that “any reader will work.” Compatibility can depend on the controller, firmware, power, cables, reader generation, provider requirements, and the machine's exact configuration.

Confirm compatibility with the payment system you actually intend to use.

Include reader purchase or rental, installation, transaction fees, monthly fees, connectivity, and software in the operating model rather than treating the reader as a decorative upgrade.

## Check refrigeration as a system

For refrigerated machines, “the cabinet feels cold” is not enough.

Consider:

- pull-down performance;
- operating temperature;
- compressor behavior;
- fans;
- door seals;
- excessive ice;
- abnormal noise.

If refrigeration condition matters to the purchase and you cannot assess it competently, obtain a technician inspection.

Fresh or temperature-sensitive food also creates operating and regulatory responsibilities beyond the refrigeration unit itself.

## Confirm physical fit and delivery

A machine has to reach the final location, not merely fit once it gets there.

Record machine width, depth, height, weight where relevant, doorway clearance, turning space, stairs, elevators, loading arrangements, and final service access.

Use the manufacturer's electrical requirements and avoid improvised power arrangements.

A cheap machine that needs two moves, storage, a difficult delivery, repairs, new payment hardware, locks, and cleaning may no longer be cheap.

## Build the deployed-cost number before agreeing a price

List the likely costs separately:

- purchase;
- transport;
- storage;
- cleaning;
- locks and keys;
- known repairs;
- payment upgrade;
- installation.

Then compare the installed cost with the economics of the qualified location.

A machine can be fairly priced in the equipment market and still be too expensive for the site you have.
