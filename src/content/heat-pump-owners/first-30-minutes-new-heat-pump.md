A new heat pump can feel complicated because the installer may have shown you several screens, multiple temperatures, and more than one control in a short handover.

The most useful first step is not to optimise anything. It is to preserve the starting point.

## 1. Identify the outdoor unit

Find the manufacturer, model, and serial number on the accessible external label. Do not remove panels.

Take a clear photograph and save the details somewhere you can find later. The exact model matters because menus and owner instructions can differ even within the same brand.

## 2. Map the indoor equipment

Record what you can see inside the property.

Depending on the installation, that may include:

- an indoor hydraulic unit;
- an integrated cylinder unit;
- a separate hot-water cylinder;
- pumps and valves;
- a buffer or volumiser;
- room thermostats;
- one or more zone controls.

You do not need to name every component correctly on day one. The point is to create a map of what exists without opening covers.

## 3. Work out which control is which

The room thermostat is not necessarily the main heat-pump controller.

You may have:

- a controller on the indoor unit;
- a wall-mounted heat-pump controller;
- a room thermostat;
- separate zone thermostats;
- a phone app.

A useful question is: **which control talks directly to the heat pump, and which controls simply request heat?**

If you do not yet know, record what each control is called and where it is.

## 4. Find the paperwork

Keep the available documents together in one physical or digital folder.

Look for:

- user manuals;
- controller instructions;
- commissioning information;
- installer handover documents;
- warranty information;
- design information supplied at installation;
- hot-water cylinder instructions;
- smart-control instructions;
- installer and service contact details.

If the installation is MCS-certified, keep the MCS certificate with the rest of the heat-pump records.

## 5. Record the visible starting settings

Read the controller without trying to improve it.

Useful things to record, where displayed, include:

- desired room temperature;
- actual room temperature;
- space-heating mode;
- hot-water mode and target;
- schedule status;
- holiday mode;
- weather-compensation indication;
- flow or leaving-water temperature;
- outdoor temperature;
- warning or fault messages.

Do not enter protected or installer menus simply to fill a gap. **Unknown** is a perfectly useful answer.

Photographs of the main screens are often the easiest way to preserve the initial state.

## 6. Identify how each room is heated

Walk around the home and note whether rooms use:

- radiators;
- underfloor heating;
- fan convectors;
- another emitter type.

Also record whether rooms belong to different heating zones or use separate thermostats.

This distinction becomes important later. One cold bedroom is a different problem from a whole house that never reaches its target.

## 7. Find your electricity information

Note what energy information you actually have.

That might be:

- whole-house smart-meter data;
- a supplier app;
- a dedicated heat-pump electricity meter;
- a heat meter;
- energy information on the heat-pump controller.

Do not assume every number labelled “energy” means the same thing. Whole-house electricity, heat-pump electricity, and heat delivered are different measurements.

## Your handover reset

Before making changes, try to answer these questions:

- What outdoor unit do I have?
- What indoor unit and hot-water equipment do I have?
- Where is the main heat-pump controller?
- Which room controls do I normally use?
- Do I have one heating zone or several?
- Are the emitters radiators, underfloor heating, or both?
- Do I have the correct user documentation?
- Do I have installer or service contact details?
- Have I recorded the visible starting settings?
- Have I photographed the starting configuration?

You do not need the “perfect” flow temperature or heating curve yet.

A reliable baseline is more useful than a collection of settings copied from somebody else's system.
