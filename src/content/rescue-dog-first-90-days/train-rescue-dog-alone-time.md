A newly adopted dog can seem comfortable while you are in the room and become distressed as soon as you leave.

The worst time to discover that is at the beginning of a long workday.

Alone-time training starts by observing **small, safe separations** before you need a long absence.

## Distress is not disobedience

Possible signs during absence include:

- persistent vocalizing;
- frantic pacing;
- scratching or biting at exits;
- heavy drooling;
- inability to eat a normally valued food item;
- repeated escape attempts;
- destruction focused around exits;
- toileting associated with panic.

A dog who is panicking is not learning that crying “does not work.”

Repeatedly forcing long absences can rehearse the distress.

Severe or persistent separation-related behavior deserves veterinary and qualified behavior support.

## Observe before you assume

During the early days, test tiny separations only when the environment is safe.

Examples include:

- stand up and sit down while the dog rests;
- walk several steps away and return;
- move briefly behind a gate;
- disappear around a corner for a few seconds;
- open and close the front door without leaving;
- step outside for a very short period.

Use a camera when possible.

A quiet dog when you return does not tell you what happened while you were gone.

## Start below the point where the dog becomes worried

Think of duration as a ladder.

One example might be:

1. dog rests while you stand up and sit down;
2. you move three steps away and return;
3. you go behind a gate for two seconds;
4. you disappear around a corner for five seconds;
5. you open and close the front door;
6. you step outside for three seconds;
7. you step outside for ten seconds;
8. you go to the end of the path and return;
9. you leave for one minute;
10. duration grows in small, non-linear increments.

You do not need to climb one rung on every repetition.

Mix easier repetitions with slightly harder ones.

If the dog shows concern, return to a level that was comfortable.

## Keep departure cues boring

Keys, shoes, coats, bags, and door locks can become predictors of absence.

During early practice, sometimes:

- pick up the keys and sit down;
- put on shoes and make a drink;
- open the front door and close it;
- carry a bag into another room.

The goal is to reduce the contrast between ordinary household activity and the moment you disappear.

Keep departures and returns calm enough to repeat.

## Food toys can give information, but they are not a cure

A food toy can make a short absence pleasant for a dog who is comfortable enough to eat.

If the dog normally values the item but abandons it when you leave, that is useful information.

The absence may already be too difficult.

Do not use food to conceal distress you would otherwise notice.

## Crate comfort and alone time are separate skills

A crate does not treat separation distress.

Some dogs are already comfortable in a crate. Others become more distressed when confinement is added to absence.

Train crate comfort separately.

If the dog shows intense escape behavior, heavy drooling, self-injury risk, or panic in confinement, stop treating the crate as the default answer and speak to a veterinarian or behavior professional.

## Keep a simple log

You do not need a complicated spreadsheet.

Record:

- duration;
- setup;
- what the dog did;
- what the next step should be.

For example:

**10 seconds behind gate:** dog looked up and returned to chew → repeat or try 15 seconds.

**45 seconds with front door closed:** dog stood at door and stopped eating → make the next repetition easier.

**2 minutes on camera:** dog paced and vocalized continuously → stop increasing duration and seek advice if the pattern repeats.

Progress is the ability to remain comfortable, not the number on the clock.

## What if you must leave before training is ready?

Management is part of the plan.

Depending on the dog and household, that may include:

- a trusted friend or family member;
- a pet sitter;
- temporary work-from-home arrangements;
- shift changes;
- support from the rescue organization.

Daycare is not automatically the answer for a newly adopted dog, especially one who is fearful, reactive, overwhelmed by groups, or medically unsettled.

If unavoidable absences are repeatedly longer than the dog can handle, get professional help early.

Waiting for the dog to “get used to it” is not a training plan.

## Know when to escalate

Seek veterinary or qualified behavior help when:

- distress is severe or persistent;
- escape attempts create injury risk;
- the dog cannot eat or settle when left;
- required household absences greatly exceed the dog's current ability;
- confinement makes the response worse;
- the pattern is preventing normal household function.
