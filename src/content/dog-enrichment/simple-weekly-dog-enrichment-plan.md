A useful dog enrichment routine does not need a packed calendar. It is easier to maintain when a few familiar activities cover most ordinary days.

A simple starting point from *Busy Dog, Better Day* is a **three-anchor week**.

## Anchor A: sniff

Choose one scent-led activity that is already easy to set up.

That might be Towel Search, Room Scatter, Toy Search, or another familiar search. Use it more than once during the week rather than assuming repetition makes the activity less valuable.

You can change the room, visibility, distance, or another single variable when the current version becomes too easy.

## Anchor B: think or train

Choose one short problem-solving or familiar-cue game.

Two-Bowl Choice, Object Touch, Toy Names, and One-Cue Puzzle are examples from the book. Keep the session short enough that the dog is still willing to engage at the end.

A thinking game should not be a repeated test. If the dog starts guessing, stalls, or becomes frustrated, return to a clearer version.

## Anchor C: move or play

Choose one food-free or low-food option that suits the dog's physical comfort and play style.

Mat-to-Mat Walk, Slow Follow, Tug With Pauses, and Roll-and-Return can fill this role. Movement should stay controlled, with non-slip flooring, broad turns, and no pressure to create intensity.

For dogs with medical or exercise restrictions, follow appropriate veterinary guidance rather than turning enrichment into improvised rehabilitation.

## On busy days, count five minutes

Do not “make up” a missed session by piling several difficult activities into the next day.

A simple five-minute game can count. Ordinary walks, sniffing, social contact, rest, and sleep are part of the dog's life too. Enrichment should fit around those things rather than compete with them.

If you want a short list, start with the [five-minute dog enrichment guide](/dog-enrichment/five-minute-dog-enrichment-games/).

## On high-energy days, do not assume the answer is more speed

A physically active dog can still benefit from slow scent work, searching, and problem-solving.

Instead of automatically adding more fast throwing, tight turning, or repetitive hard fetch, pair the normal routine with a calm search or a short thinking game.

The purpose is not to exhaust the dog. It is to provide useful outlets such as sniffing, searching, chewing safely, solving, moving, choosing, playing, and interacting.

## On lower-energy days, make the activity gentler

Use familiar nose work, a toy choice, a quiet observation activity, or another easy puzzle that does not demand speed.

A sudden or unusual drop in energy is a health question rather than an enrichment challenge. Persistent appetite change, pain, unusual fatigue, or a marked behavior change deserves appropriate veterinary attention.

## When the routine feels stale, change context first

Before buying another puzzle or inventing a more elaborate setup, change one small part of a known activity.

Use Towel Search in another room. Rotate a familiar toy back into use. Reverse a safe landmark loop. Offer the activity at a different time of day.

Novelty can come from context, sequence, and availability rather than constant accumulation.

## Start with a realistic target

If deliberate enrichment is new to the household, three or four sessions in the first week can be enough to learn what is easy to prepare and what the dog actually chooses.

After two or three weeks, identify the few activities that give you the best combination of easy setup and good engagement. Those become the default menu.

A routine you actually use is more valuable than an elaborate plan abandoned after a few days.
