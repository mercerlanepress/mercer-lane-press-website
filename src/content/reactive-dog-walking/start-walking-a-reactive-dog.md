A reactive walk can feel as though everything goes wrong at once. Your dog notices something, the leash tightens, distance disappears, barking or lunging starts, and the rest of the outing becomes an attempt to prevent the next reaction.

A more useful starting point is to split the problem into separate jobs:

1. **Keep everyone safe.**
2. **Reduce how often the dog is pushed beyond coping capacity.**
3. **Teach a small set of skills that are available before the reaction becomes large.**

“Reactive” describes an observable response. It is not a diagnosis. Fear, frustration, over-arousal, learning history, pain, or several factors together can produce similar-looking behavior. On a walk, you can still make better decisions by tracking what you can actually observe: the trigger, distance, body-language changes, recovery time, and whether the dog can move, eat, sniff, and turn away.

## Redefine success for the first week

Do not make “walk past every dog calmly” the first target. A more workable goal is a walk in which you notice problems earlier and leave before they become unmanageable.

That matters because repeated full-strength reactions are repeated practice of the very pattern you want to reduce. Management is therefore part of the training system, not a sign that training has failed.

For the first several days, write down only what will change decisions:

- the three most common triggers;
- the approximate distance at which your dog can notice each one and still respond;
- the first body-language change you usually see;
- how long recovery takes after a difficult encounter;
- one lower-traffic route and one backup route.

You do not need exact measurements. “Across a two-lane road,” “three parked cars,” or “the far side of the small park” can be useful anchors if you apply them consistently.

## Choose a non-negotiable exit rule

Decide before the walk when you will create distance.

A simple rule is: if the dog stops taking normally valued food, cannot turn away, becomes increasingly fixed on the trigger, or is otherwise escalating, **make space immediately**.

Food is useful information but not a perfect threshold test. Some dogs will continue eating while highly aroused. Look at the whole dog: posture, movement, breathing, scanning, and the ability to disengage.

Your exit can be a turn, a safe road crossing, an arc away, a retreat to the last wider area, or movement behind a car, hedge, wall, tree, or building corner. Practice the movement when no trigger is present so the cue already has a history of easy success.

## Use a 14-day reset rather than testing limits

The first two weeks do not need to be an intensive exposure program. Use easier routes, practice orientation and turn-away skills away from triggers, identify barriers, and gather enough data to see patterns.

During the second week, add a few planned observations at generous distance. Leave while the dog is still successful. The purpose is to find a repeatable setup, not the minimum distance the dog can tolerate.

At the end of the reset, choose one initial trigger for structured work. Do not turn every trigger into a project at once.

## What to practice before the next difficult walk

Four simple skills cover many walking situations:

- orient toward you;
- move toward or touch a presented hand;
- search for scattered food on safe ground;
- turn away and move with you.

Teach them first where nothing important is happening. The aim is not perfect obedience. The aim is to create useful, well-reinforced behaviors that remain available when the environment becomes a little harder.
