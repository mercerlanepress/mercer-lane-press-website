A dink sits a little higher than the one before it.

You *could* speed it up.

That does not mean you should.

The useful distinction is **opportunity versus obligation**.

## Run the attackability test

Before increasing pace, read more than height.

### Height

Can you contact the ball at a height that gives a useful attacking window?

Higher contact generally creates more margin, but it is only one factor.

### Balance

Can you strike without reaching, falling, or improvising?

A high ball while you are leaning sideways can be a worse attack than a slightly lower ball that you can contact cleanly in front.

### Strike zone

Is the ball positioned far enough from your body for controlled contact?

A ball jammed into your shoulder or hip can remove options even if it is technically high.

### Opponent readiness

Are the opponents balanced and waiting?

Or is one player moving, reaching, separated, leaning, or recovering?

An opponent's loss of readiness can make a moderate ball more useful.

### Partner readiness

If the ball is countered, is your partner prepared?

That question converts an individual attack into a doubles decision.

## Add one more question: what if it comes back?

At NVZ distance, many good attacks are returned.

Your plan should not end at the first speed-up.

Ask where the likely counter can go and what space the attack changes.

Both partners should enter counter-ready mode.

## Know why you are speeding up

A speed-up can have different jobs.

**Finish:** exploit a clearly favorable ball with a realistic open target.

**Create:** produce a weaker counter that gives your team the next attack.

**Disrupt:** change a comfortable pattern when you have a sufficiently good ball and enough margin.

Expecting every first speed-up to be a winner encourages overswinging and poor preparation.

Often the better sequence is:

**attack → expect ball back → use the next ball**

## Do not attack because the rally feels long

The number of previous dinks does not change the geometry of the next ball.

If both opponents remain balanced and the ball stays low, impatience has not created a new tactical fact.

Create one.

Move an opponent.

Change direction deliberately.

Use controlled depth.

Jam an awkward contact point.

Then recognize the opening when it actually appears.

## Choose a target, not just more pace

Once an attack is justified, decide what problem you want the opponent to solve.

Possible targets include:

- the feet of a moving or deeper player;
- the middle, where responsibility can overlap;
- open court created by displacement;
- the body area when it makes paddle preparation awkward;
- a side that has proved uncomfortable through observation.

Maximum pace is not automatically the best attack.

Placement and readiness matter.

## If the exchange turns against you, change state

A speed-up can be correct on one ball and wrong on the next.

You attack.

The opponent counters strongly.

Now your team may be neutral or under pressure.

There is no obligation to keep hitting harder because you started the exchange.

If the opponent now owns the firefight, block or reset and rebuild.

The strategy follows the current rally, not the memory of the previous ball.

For a related team responsibility, see [how partners should move together](/pickleball-doubles/pickleball-doubles-partner-positioning/) and [who takes the middle](/pickleball-doubles/who-takes-middle-pickleball-doubles/).

The practical rule is:

**speed up because the evidence changed, then prepare for the next decision.**
