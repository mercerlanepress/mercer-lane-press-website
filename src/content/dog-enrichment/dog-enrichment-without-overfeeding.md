Food is convenient for enrichment because many dogs value it and it can be divided into small rewards. The common mistake is letting "enrichment food" sit outside the normal feeding plan.

*Dog Enrichment for Apartments & Busy Owners* uses a simple default: **reserve part of the dog's normal meal for enrichment instead of automatically adding treats on top**.

## Measure the day before the game

A practical routine is:

1. measure the day's food as usual;
2. set aside the portion you expect to use for searches, training, or food toys;
3. use small pieces for training-style activities;
4. account for wet food or other approved food in the overall plan;
5. follow veterinary guidance for medical diets, weight concerns, or other individual nutrition needs.

The point of enrichment is the searching, manipulating, licking, learning, or interaction. A larger food quantity does not automatically create a better activity.

## Use meals in different ways

The same measured food can create very different experiences.

A few examples from the book:

- **Kibble Constellation:** spread a small measured portion across easy visible spots in a clean safe area.
- **Treat Trail to Treasure:** use a short, obvious food trail with a clear finish.
- **Muffin-Tin Forage:** begin with food in uncovered wells, then add only a small amount of simple object movement.
- **Kibble in a Baking Tray:** spread food across a wider flat surface instead of piling it in a bowl.
- **Slow Scatter Breakfast:** divide one meal into several small scatters with pauses between them.
- **Stuffed Rubber Toy: Easy Fill:** pack part of the meal loosely enough that the first attempt succeeds without frantic chewing.

The activity changes how the dog gets the food, not necessarily how much food the dog receives.

## Difficulty should not come from withholding food

If the dog becomes frantic, bites equipment, paws repeatedly without a workable strategy, or gives up, making the puzzle even harder is rarely useful.

Make the food easier to obtain. Reveal more of it, open the puzzle, reduce the number of moving parts, or switch to a simple floor or tray search.

For a dog that is highly food-focused, easy success can be safer and more useful than creating an extraction contest.

## Choose foods already known to be appropriate

The manuscript deliberately avoids recommending random human foods simply because they are easy to spread or freeze.

Use food already known to be safe for the individual dog and follow professional guidance where the dog has a medical diet, allergies, weight concerns, dental issues, or another relevant condition.

Common household hazards are not enrichment ingredients. The book specifically flags xylitol, grapes and raisins, chocolate, onions and garlic, and alcohol as foods to avoid.

## Be cautious with frozen and long-duration food activities

A **Frozen Bowl Lick** uses a thin layer rather than a deep solid block. The goal is a longer licking activity, not hard biting at a frozen mass.

A **Lick Mat: Thin Smear** uses a small amount over part of the surface and is removed if the dog begins chewing the mat.

A **Stuffed Rubber Toy** should be size-appropriate, intact, and supervised until the dog's use is known. No food toy is indestructible.

## Multi-dog homes may need separate setups

A simple food puzzle can become a conflict if dogs compete over it.

The book advises separating animals for high-value activities when needed and giving each dog an opportunity that suits that dog. "Fair" does not have to mean that every dog receives the identical setup.

## Food is optional

Some dogs prefer toy search, social interaction, observation, chewing, choice, targeting, or controlled movement.

If food use is inconvenient or inappropriate, switch categories. The [no-food indoor enrichment guide](/dog-enrichment/no-food-indoor-dog-enrichment-games/) gives alternatives that do not depend on extra calories.
