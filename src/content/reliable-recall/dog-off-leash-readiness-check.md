Off-leash readiness is not a permanent certificate.

A dog can be ready in a secure field and not ready on a woodland trail. The same dog can be successful on an empty beach and struggle when birds, running dogs, wind, or food scraps change the picture.

A better question is:

**What evidence do I have for this place, under today's conditions?**

## Name the exact environment

Do not assess “off leash” in the abstract.

Write down the setting you are considering:

- fenced field;
- open meadow;
- beach;
- public park;
- woodland trail;
- campsite;
- another legal area relevant to your life.

Then identify the distractions that are normally present there.

Evidence from one environment does not automatically transfer to another.

## Check six practical conditions

Before unclipping in a legal, low-risk setting, look for evidence that:

1. **First-cue returns have been repeated** in this environment or in genuinely similar conditions.
2. **The relevant distraction class has been proofed**—for example other dogs, scent, people, birds, or movement.
3. **The dog comes all the way into handling reach**, rather than stopping short or swerving away.
4. **Leash clipping is routine**, without the dog dodging your hands or avoiding the equipment.
5. **The dog checks in and stays connected**, rather than immediately ranging beyond the distance you have trained.
6. **One mistake is unlikely to become catastrophic.**

If an essential part is unknown or inconsistent, keep the line on and train the missing piece.

## Run a consequence check

Ask what happens if the recall fails once.

In a secure private field, the consequence may be inconvenience. Near traffic, livestock, cliffs, fragile wildlife, fast water, uncontrolled exits, or other serious hazards, one error can carry far greater cost.

The higher the consequence, the more conservative the decision should be. Some places should remain leash or long-line environments regardless of training progress.

Local leash, wildlife, access, and site rules also override training readiness.

## Require repeated evidence, not one perfect day

One exceptional outing is weak evidence.

Look for first-cue returns across several sessions, including ordinary changes in the environment. The dog should still come into reach and accept the leash clip when excitement has not been allowed to escalate beyond the trained level.

A readiness decision should be based on the dog's current performance, not a historical best.

## Make the first trial easier than the last long-line session

If the gate is passed, do not celebrate by choosing the hardest environment.

Use a familiar, legal, low-risk place at a quiet time. Let the dog settle into the environment first. Keep the first period of freedom short.

A simple first block can be:

- warm up on leash or long line;
- unclip for a minute or two;
- reinforce voluntary check-ins;
- make one easy recall;
- reward;
- re-leash while performance is still strong.

Later sessions can lengthen gradually.

## Re-leash before difficulty spikes

Do not wait until the dog is already over-aroused, deep in a scent trail, chasing, or absorbed by another dog before trying to end the session.

Watch the environment as well as the dog. Re-leash proactively when:

- wildlife appears;
- another dog is approaching;
- sightlines become poor;
- distance starts expanding beyond your evidence;
- the dog stops checking in;
- food interest disappears with rising arousal;
- a crowd, game, or other new distraction changes the setting.

Proactive management is part of good recall handling, not evidence that training failed.

## One ignored cue changes the plan

If a recall fails during an off-leash trial, secure the dog calmly when possible. Do not punish the eventual return.

Treat the event as evidence that one readiness condition was overestimated. Return to managed proofing in that context until the missing level has been rebuilt.

Freedom is earned by context and can be reduced again when the context changes.
