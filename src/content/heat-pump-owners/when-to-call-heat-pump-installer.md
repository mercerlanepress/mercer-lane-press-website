Good heat-pump troubleshooting does not mean continuing until you personally find the fault.

It means knowing where normal owner checks end and professional work begins.

The aim is to stop at the right point with useful information.

## Four useful categories

### 1. Normal owner-control issue

The equipment appears healthy and the cause is something like:

- a room target;
- a schedule;
- a setback;
- holiday mode;
- a temporary override;
- another ordinary user control.

These are generally **Green** owner actions.

### 2. System-specific configuration question

The system may need an adjustment, but the correct answer depends on how it was designed and commissioned.

Examples can include:

- weather compensation;
- leaving-water-temperature strategy;
- domestic-hot-water configuration;
- backup-heater behaviour;
- interaction between different controls or zones.

This is often **Amber**.

If the next step requires a protected installer or commissioning menu, stop.

### 3. Installation or equipment problem

Examples can include:

- persistent inability to maintain reasonable comfort;
- unexplained auxiliary heating;
- recurring fault codes;
- a whole zone that behaves incorrectly despite normal user settings;
- an emitter or distribution problem;
- a sudden significant change after months of stable operation.

These need a more focused professional investigation.

### 4. Safety or repair issue

Internal electrical work, refrigerant work, safety devices, significant leaks, dismantling, and repair or commissioning work are **Red**.

These are not owner troubleshooting tasks.

## The stop test

Ask yourself:

- Do I understand the user control involved?
- Is the next adjustment documented as an owner action?
- Have I already made several adjustments without improvement?
- Is a fault repeatedly returning?
- Does the issue involve internal electrical, refrigerant, pressure, safety, or repair work?
- Is the house repeatedly uncomfortable despite sensible ordinary settings?
- Is electricity use repeatedly abnormal without a credible explanation?
- Would the next step require a protected installer menu?

If the next useful step is no longer an ordinary owner action, stop changing things.

## Preserve the fault before resetting

When a warning or fault appears, record:

- date and time;
- displayed message;
- exact fault code;
- operating mode;
- what stopped working;
- a photograph of the controller if practical.

Then use the exact model information from your system record and check the official manufacturer user documentation.

Do not repeatedly create a cycle of:

**fault → reset → fault → reset**

If a manufacturer provides a specific user-level recovery procedure, follow that procedure. A recurring fault is evidence worth preserving.

## Sudden change without a fault code

Not every genuine problem creates an error message.

Use the same discipline when a previously stable system suddenly:

- cannot maintain temperature;
- uses much more electricity;
- produces inadequate hot water;
- relies heavily on auxiliary heat;
- develops a new unusual noise.

Compare **before** with **after**.

Record current settings, outdoor conditions, recent changes, and anything unusual that happened before the change.

## Who should you contact?

### The installer

Usually the first choice for:

- commissioning questions;
- unclear system configuration;
- persistent comfort problems;
- heating-curve questions;
- suspected installation issues.

### Manufacturer or authorised service network

Useful for:

- equipment-specific faults;
- warranty questions;
- technical service support;
- locating authorised engineers.

### Another appropriately competent professional

Where the issue genuinely falls within that person's area of work.

The useful principle is to match the professional to the problem.

## Make the call useful

A strong first sentence follows this structure:

**The system normally ______, but since ______ it has ______.**

For example:

> The system normally maintains the living room around its target, but since last week it has remained several degrees below target during colder weather despite the normal schedule.

Bring:

- outdoor and indoor unit model information;
- controller model;
- normal room target;
- actual room temperature;
- outdoor conditions;
- fault code or message;
- recent setting changes;
- evidence showing the pattern.

Useful questions include:

- Is this behaviour consistent with how the system was commissioned?
- Is the current heating curve what was intended at handover?
- Why is the auxiliary heater operating this frequently?
- Does the fault history suggest an equipment or installation issue?
- Is this setting intended for the householder to adjust?
- What should I monitor after the change?

## Record professional changes

After a visit, ask what changed.

Record:

- the date;
- the finding;
- settings or components changed;
- the reason;
- what to watch next.

That becomes the new baseline instead of letting the system's history disappear.
