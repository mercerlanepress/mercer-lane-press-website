A dog enrichment activity does not need to be replaced as soon as the dog understands it. The same game can change through a few simple variables.

The key rule is to change **one variable at a time**. That makes it easier to see what changed the dog's response and just as easy to reverse the change.

## Visibility

More visible is easier. More fully hidden is harder.

A toy can begin half-visible behind a chair before being hidden completely. Food can begin on top of a towel before being placed inside loose folds.

If a dog stops searching and begins looking to you for the answer, increase visibility before adding more pointing or prompting.

## Distance

Closer is easier. Farther away is harder.

This applies to toy searches, targets, mats, person searches, and food trails. Increase distance in small steps rather than making the object both farther away and harder to see.

## Number of objects

Fewer objects usually make the problem clearer.

One cup with an access gap is easier than several cups. One obvious target is easier than several plausible targets. Extra safe decoys can add difficulty after the dog understands the underlying game.

## Scent strength

A larger or more aromatic food reward can make a scent problem easier. A familiar toy that has just been played with may be easier to follow than a faint scent source.

Do not confuse weaker scent with better enrichment. The goal is a workable search, not the smallest possible clue.

## Space

A small search zone is easier to understand than several rooms.

Room Scatter can begin in one open area before spreading pieces across a larger safe room. A toy search can begin behind one obvious furniture edge before using a wider area.

Clear the space before the dog starts. More space should not mean more hazards.

## Delay

An immediate search is easier than a search after a pause.

Delay is most useful when the dog already understands the game. In a simple memory activity, a reward may be placed behind a landmark while the dog watches, followed by only a three-second pause before the dog returns to look for it.

If the dog forgets repeatedly, shorten the delay rather than rehearsing failure.

## Choice load

One obvious option is easier. Two or three plausible options require more figuring out.

A two-bowl choice can begin with the rewarded bowl easy to identify. A toy-name exercise should begin with one toy before two different toys are presented together.

More choices are useful only when the basic relationship is already clear.

## Movement demand

Short, straight, and slow is easier.

Longer routes, gentle direction changes, or extra landmarks can add complexity, but physical risk should never be the mechanism used to make a game challenging. Avoid turning broad walking routes into tight pivots, raised obstacles, unstable surfaces, or repeated hard stops.

## Watch for the direction the dog needs

Difficulty is not a one-way ladder.

If the dog stays engaged, can pause, and willingly returns to the activity, the level may be appropriate. If the setup creates frantic grabbing, repeated avoidance, equipment-chewing, distress, pain, or another safety concern, simplify or end.

A hard version today can become an easy version tomorrow after a tiring day, a noisy environment, a change in routine, or another shift in context.

The aim is not to reach the hardest version. It is to keep the activity understandable, safe, and worth returning to.
