The goal in doubles is often to establish both partners in a useful position near the non-volley-zone line.

That does not make the journey a race.

A common mistake is to hit a reasonable drop, see the ball travel toward the kitchen, and keep running while the opponent is already preparing a firm volley at the feet.

Another mistake is to stay deep until a drop looks perfect.

Both approaches replace reading the rally with a fixed instruction.

## Use one transition rule

A practical default is:

**Move when your ball gives you time.**

**Stop when their contact takes your time away.**

If your drop makes an opponent reach below net height, you may have earned movement.

If your drive produces a weak floating block, you may have earned movement.

If an opponent is balanced and about to contact a high or comfortable ball, stop.

If a hard ball is travelling toward your feet, deal with the ball before worrying about your destination.

## Read green, red, and amber signals

### Green: advance

Movement is more attractive when the opponent is:

- reaching below net height;
- stretching;
- moving backward;
- contacting a soft low ball;
- forced to let the ball bounce;
- otherwise unlikely to attack strongly.

The exact number of steps is not fixed. Take the ground the ball actually earned.

### Red: stop and prepare

Stop when the opponent is:

- balanced;
- contacting high;
- stepping into an aggressive volley;
- receiving a floating ball;
- likely to send pace toward your feet.

Your priority has changed from gaining court to being able to play the next ball.

### Amber: get balanced and read

Sometimes the evidence is mixed.

Slow down. Establish balance. Watch the contact.

You can continue forward after the next controlled ball if the rally allows it.

## Do not run through opponent contact

Forward motion is valuable until it prevents you from reacting.

As the opponent prepares to strike, you need feet that can respond left, right, forward, backward, or down toward a low ball.

The point is not a theatrical split step.

The point is balance when the incoming ball requires a decision.

If stopping leaves you in transition for another ball, that is not failure.

## Use the reset to change rally state

A reset is useful when the opponents have attacking leverage and your team needs to move from pressure toward neutral.

The objective is generally to absorb enough pace to regain control and send a low ball that makes another aggressive downward contact more difficult.

Do not judge the reset only by whether it lands in the kitchen.

Judge what the opponent can do next.

A successful reset may buy two steps, another reset, or a chance to reconnect with your partner.

## Advance in stages

The realistic sequence may be:

**baseline → transition → transition → NVZ**

rather than one uninterrupted run.

One good ball earns one piece of court.

Another controlled ball earns another.

A poor ball earns nothing.

The last two feet do not receive special permission. If the opponent is about to strike and you need to stop, stop.

## Arrive as a team

The destination is not "I reached the kitchen."

The useful outcome is both partners establishing a defendable forward relationship with balance, spacing, and readiness.

If one player is already forward while the other remains deep, the immediate task may be to stabilize rather than force symmetry. The forward player can protect realistic space and give the deeper partner time to work forward.

See [how doubles partners should move together](/pickleball-doubles/pickleball-doubles-partner-positioning/) for the connected-coverage principle.

A simple review question after a transition rally is:

**What ball gave us permission to move, and where should we have stopped?**

That turns movement into evidence instead of habit.
