A useful home inspection finding has a simple job: help the reader understand what was observed, where it was observed, why it matters when that is not obvious, and what should happen next.

That can be checked with four questions:

**Location → Condition → Significance → Next Action**

The framework is not a demand for four sentences. A routine finding may answer all four in one line. A more complex finding may need several sentences because uncertainty, limitations, or context matter.

## 1. Make the condition findable

Ask whether someone who was not at the inspection could locate the condition from the text.

Use enough detail to remove ambiguity, but stop when extra location words stop helping. Choose an orientation and room-naming convention and keep it stable throughout the report.

## 2. Lead with the observed condition

Start with what the inspection established.

A broad label such as “roof issue,” “damaged siding,” or “window defective” hides useful information. Describe the visible state, measured result, or operating behavior instead.

This also helps keep causes separate from observations. Staining can be visible without the inspection establishing whether the source is current plumbing leakage, roofing leakage, condensation, or a previous event.

When the source was not determined, preserve that uncertainty.

## 3. Explain significance only as far as the evidence supports

Significance connects the condition to the next action.

It may involve performance, safety, continued deterioration, or possible damage to another component. The explanation should be proportionate.

Avoid building a catastrophe chain from a modest observation. Explain the relevant mechanism when it helps the client understand why action makes sense. If the reason is already obvious, another sentence may not be needed.

## 4. Give the reader a usable next action

A recommendation should follow from the finding rather than from a generic ending stored in a template.

Common recommendation functions include:

- **Correct** — repair, replace, secure, adjust, or otherwise correct the condition.
- **Evaluate** — obtain appropriate expertise or diagnostic work when the inspection did not establish enough information.
- **Monitor** — observe a condition over time when that is professionally appropriate.
- **Maintain** — perform routine or preventive work.
- **Obtain information** — seek records, service history, manuals, permits, or other relevant information.

Avoid using “further evaluation” automatically. The correct next action depends on the actual condition, uncertainty, scope, and applicable requirements.

## Use minimum sufficient communication

Shorter is not automatically better, and longer is not automatically safer.

A concise finding is strong when the reader can locate the condition, understand what happened, understand the relevant significance, and know what to do next.

Long findings become weaker when property-specific information is buried under generic education, repeated disclaimers, possible causes, or consequences that were not established.

A practical editing rule is to ask whether every sentence performs at least one useful job: locate, describe, explain significance, communicate a limitation or uncertainty, recommend action, clarify priority, or interpret a photograph.

## A final four-question check

Before accepting the narrative, ask:

1. Can the reader find it?
2. Do they know what was actually observed?
3. Do they understand why it matters when that is not self-evident?
4. Do they know the appropriate next action?

Then check that certainty, extent, urgency, and any referral language still match the inspection.

The structure is useful precisely because it stays invisible. The client should not see a formula. They should see a clear, property-specific finding.
