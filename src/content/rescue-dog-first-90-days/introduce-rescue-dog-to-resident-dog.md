A successful introduction between a newly adopted rescue dog and a resident dog is not measured by whether they play on day one.

The first target is **calm coexistence**.

That changes the setup. You do not need to create instant friendship. You need enough space, management, and information to prevent the dogs from being pushed into conflict.

## Prepare the home before the meeting

Remove obvious pressure points.

Set up:

- separate feeding areas;
- separate beds and resting places;
- more than one water access point where practical;
- gates or closed doors for separation;
- a rotation plan if the dogs need time apart.

Pick up high-value chews and prized toys until you know how both dogs behave around resources.

Do not rely on verbal cues as the only safety layer.

## Use two competent handlers when possible

A first outdoor meeting is easier to manage when each dog has a calm handler.

Choose a neutral or low-ownership area when practical.

Use secure equipment and enough space that the dogs do not have to meet nose-to-nose immediately.

The goal of the first minutes is not touching.

It is observing whether both dogs can move, sniff, and disengage.

## Start with distance and parallel movement

Walk in the same general direction with space between the dogs.

Parallel movement can reduce the social pressure created by a direct approach.

Adjust distance gradually only while both dogs remain able to:

- move normally;
- look away;
- respond to handlers;
- sniff;
- recover from looking at the other dog.

Avoid tight lead tension and forced face-to-face greetings.

Do not hold two tense dogs close together and wait for them to “sort it out.”

## Read the interaction instead of applying a label

Words such as “dominant,” “submissive,” or “friendly” are often too broad to tell you what to do next.

Ask observable questions:

- are bodies loose or stiff?
- can each dog move away?
- do approaches curve or charge directly?
- does play pause naturally?
- do both dogs re-engage by choice?
- is one dog trying to escape?
- can both respond to their handlers?
- does one repeatedly block doors, people, beds, or resources?

The ability to pause and disengage is valuable.

## Entering the home is a new part of the introduction

A good outdoor walk does not automatically mean the indoor setup is easy.

Doorways are narrow and the home contains resources.

Depending on the dogs, it may be easier to bring them inside separately and allow visual access through a gate before a short shared period.

Keep the first indoor session brief.

Feed separately.

Keep high-value items separated.

Do not leave the dogs together unsupervised because the first hour went well.

## Interrupt early rather than dramatically

If arousal begins to rise, give the dogs a break before the situation becomes a fight.

Possible options include:

- calling them apart;
- guiding them behind separate barriers;
- ending play;
- scattering food separately if doing so is safe for those dogs;
- increasing space.

Do not wait for snarling or fighting before deciding the session has gone on long enough.

Do not punish growling. Treat it as information that the setup needs changing.

## Expect the picture to change after the first day

A newly adopted dog may be inhibited initially and show more behavior as the home becomes familiar.

That does not automatically mean the dog has changed personality.

Reassess routines and resources if conflict appears later.

Common pressure points include:

- doorways;
- human attention;
- food preparation;
- sofas;
- high-value items;
- fence lines;
- over-aroused play.

Keep management available even after several good sessions.

## When not to progress

Separate the dogs and seek qualified help when you see:

- repeated fights;
- bites;
- one dog repeatedly cornering or targeting the other;
- escalating guarding;
- an inability to interrupt safely;
- a substantial size or power mismatch combined with unsafe behavior.

A household can still be successful when dogs need long-term management.

“They must become best friends” is not a useful safety goal.

## A practical introduction checklist

Before increasing shared freedom, check that you have:

- separate food and high-value items;
- multiple resting areas;
- reliable barriers;
- enough distance during early meetings;
- parallel movement before close interaction;
- short supervised indoor periods;
- planned breaks;
- no unsupervised shared time until behavior has become reliably predictable.

If either dog shifts from workable behavior into repeated tension, return to an easier setup.
