The decisive evidence in a genealogy brick wall may never have been created under the target ancestor's own name.

A sibling may name the parents. A witness may turn out to be a cousin. A neighbour may reveal a migration route. A child's marriage record may preserve a mother's maiden name. A probate file may connect people who never appear together in a census.

This is the logic behind researching friends, associates, and neighbours—often shortened to FAN research. The important part is choosing the people around the target who are most likely to help answer the current research question.

## Do not research everybody

A census page may contain dozens of households. You do not need dozens of new family trees.

Prioritise people who share an unusual surname, repeatedly live nearby, appear as witnesses, sponsors, or informants, buy or sell land with the target, appear in probate or court material, share a distinctive occupation, belong to the same church, marry into the family, share a specific place of origin, migrate at roughly the same time, or appear more than once across independent records.

Repetition is a signal. It is not proof of kinship, but it can justify targeted research.

## Ask why each associate matters

For every person you add to the case, record:

- connection to the target;
- dates and places;
- whether the connection repeats;
- why this person could help answer the research question;
- next action.

If you cannot say why the person matters, do not expand the case around them yet.

## Use relatives as parallel evidence sources

Different family members preserve different information.

One sibling may have a detailed obituary. Another may have a church marriage. A third may have a death certificate supplied by a better-informed child. A sibling who remained in the original community may retain stronger links to parents, property, church, cemetery, and local records than the person who migrated.

Children can also reach backward. Their marriages, deaths, baptisms, obituaries, and other records may preserve information about a parent's maiden name, birthplace, or family connection that the parent's own records omit.

## Turn the clue into more than one explanation

Associate research becomes much more useful when you resist turning the first promising clue into the answer.

Suppose Patrick O'Brien repeatedly appears with Michael O'Brien and one record calls them brothers. Patrick's obituary names a specific locality in County Cork.

Possible hypotheses might include:

- Michael belonged to Patrick's same family in that locality;
- Michael came from the same district but belonged to another family;
- the relationship or locality evidence has been interpreted too narrowly.

Now the search has a purpose: distinguish among those explanations.

## Write the prediction before searching

For each hypothesis, ask: **If this is correct, what evidence should exist?**

Then choose the source most capable of testing that prediction.

For a parentage hypothesis, a probate file or parish baptism may discriminate better than another broad census search. For a migration hypothesis, land, tax, directory, church, or associated-family evidence may be more useful than the target's name alone.

A practical search design records the hypothesis, predicted evidence, best source, jurisdiction, date range, names and variants, search method, result, effect on the hypothesis, and next action.

## Search to challenge the preferred explanation

If one hypothesis already feels convincing, test its strongest weakness.

Ask: **What evidence would prove me wrong?**

A useful hypothesis should survive attempts to break it. If it requires you to explain away the spouse, children, occupation, residence, age, and birthplace all at once, the problem may be the hypothesis itself.

## Know when a case remains open

A successful investigation does not have to end with a new ancestor attached to the tree.

An open case can still establish which candidates are impossible, which jurisdictions have been searched, which relationships remain plausible, which records do not survive, and which evidence would matter most next.

That is progress without false certainty.
