A four-week plan is long enough to collect useful information, reduce obvious repeated problems, build several skills, and test them in easier real-life situations.

It is not a promise that a dog's behavior will be transformed in thirty days.

Move through the plan by the dog's performance, not by the calendar alone.

## Before Day 1: choose a baseline

Pick three specific target situations, such as:

- front-door arrivals;
- passing dogs on walks;
- evening mouthiness;
- settling while dinner is prepared;
- car exits;
- ending fetch;
- visitors moving around the living room.

For each target, record:

1. what the dog does now;
2. the earliest warning sign;
3. approximate recovery time;
4. current workable distance or duration;
5. management already in place.

Choose a few observable progress measures. Useful measures include frequency, intensity, workable distance, duration, recovery time, ability to eat or sniff normally, and how much owner intervention is required.

The [arousal-map guide](/over-aroused-dog/build-dog-arousal-map/) gives you a simple record format.

## Week 1: lower the noise

The first week is mainly management and observation.

### Audit repeated behavior

Identify the highest-frequency rehearsals of the behavior you want to change.

That might be window barking, demand barking, rushing the front door, dragging toward a favorite location, or jumping when someone enters the kitchen.

Make one immediate management change for each.

### Audit rest

Notice where the dog genuinely rests and what interrupts it. Window traffic, household movement, late-evening play, or a poorly placed bed may keep a dog monitoring the environment.

Protect one useful recovery window.

### Check exercise after-effects

Observe the dog ten minutes and one hour after a common activity.

If the dog is regularly more frantic afterward, change one variable: duration, intensity, location, timing, or the recovery activity that follows.

### Find one “cooling” activity

Test a low-intensity option such as sniffing, an easy food search, chewing where appropriate, or quiet exploration.

Keep what actually improves recovery.

### Catch calm behavior

Use a clear marker if one is already being introduced and quietly reinforce a few naturally calm behaviors during the day.

By the end of Week 1, the useful outcome is not perfection. It is fewer unnecessary rehearsals and a clearer understanding of the dog's early escalation signs.

## Week 2: build easy home skills

Add a small set of reliable skills in low-distraction conditions.

Useful choices include:

- voluntary returns to a mat;
- name response;
- hand target;
- a simple “this way” turn;
- a one-second pause before access to food, a door, a toy, or sniffing.

Keep sessions short.

The [settle-mat guide](/over-aroused-dog/teach-dog-settle-mat/) gives a full progression for the mat.

At the end of Week 2, ask whether the dog has several easy skills at home and whether reinforcement can happen without creating a large spike in excitement.

## Week 3: add controlled difficulty

Change one important variable at a time.

Examples:

- stand up or take one step while the dog is on the mat;
- mark voluntary check-ins on an easy outdoor walk;
- let the dog notice a mild distraction at a generous distance and reinforce disengagement;
- practice touching a door handle and cracking the door without a real visitor;
- end vigorous play one repetition earlier, then shift into sniffing;
- take one familiar skill to an easy new location for a short session.

Do not make the new setting the hardest version of the problem.

## Week 4: apply the system to the three targets

Use deliberately easier versions of the situations that matter.

A practical planning reminder is to work below the maximum difficulty you think the dog might barely manage. The book uses “about 70 percent” as a reminder to leave margin, not as a mathematical threshold.

For a visitor target, a familiar helper can enter quietly while the dog starts behind a gate.

For a walking target, choose an open location with generous passing distance.

For a play target, shorten the session enough that release and recovery are still clean.

Include no-test days when you do not deliberately expose the dog to difficult triggers.

## Compare the new baseline

At the end of the month, compare each target on:

- frequency;
- intensity;
- workable distance or duration;
- recovery time;
- owner effort;
- the dog's ability to eat, sniff, pause, or disengage.

Then write one small next-step criterion for each target.

Do not reduce distance, add duration, or increase distraction just because the calendar says it is time.

## Know when to progress

Progress when most of these are true:

- normal rewards still work;
- behavior remains flexible;
- recovery is relatively quick for this dog;
- repeated cues are not needed;
- success occurs across several sessions;
- the dog can disengage;
- the next step is small.

Stay at the current level when performance is inconsistent.

Reduce difficulty when reactions are getting bigger, recovery is getting slower, the dog stops eating or exploring, restraint is holding the setup together, avoidance is increasing, or the work feels more like testing than teaching.

## Keep the safety boundary visible

A month-long plan is for ordinary training and management, not for proving that a dog can tolerate a dangerous situation.

Sudden behavior change, suspected pain or illness, severe fear, aggression, biting or attempted biting, redirected behavior, repeated escape, or a household situation where people or animals cannot be kept safe should move the plan toward veterinary and appropriately qualified professional help.
