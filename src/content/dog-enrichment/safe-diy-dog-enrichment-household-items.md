Household items can make simple dog enrichment possible without buying a new puzzle for every activity. The useful question is not whether an object is ordinary. It is whether the specific object is suitable for the specific dog and setup.

A quick four-part check keeps DIY ideas conservative: **size, structure, surface, swallow risk**.

## 1. Size

Ask whether the whole object, or any part that could detach, can fit far enough into the dog's mouth to create a choking or swallowing risk.

If the answer is yes, do not use it. Choose a larger object or a different game.

This matters for covers placed over food, loose balls used with a muffin tin, small containers, detachable pieces, and anything a dog may pick up rather than simply nudge.

## 2. Structure

Check whether the item can collapse, tip, splinter, shatter, trap a paw, or close around the dog.

Avoid brittle plastic, glass, ceramic, unstable stacks, lightweight furniture that can tip, folding chairs that can collapse, or deep containers that encourage a dog to force the head inside.

Stable and simple is preferable to clever and precarious.

Movement games should also stay low-impact. A household obstacle is not improved by raising it, making it wobble, or creating tight routes. In *Busy Dog, Better Day*, floor markers stay flat, chairs are stable, turns stay broad, and surfaces should resist slipping.

## 3. Surface

Run a hand and visual check over the object before use.

Remove or avoid:

- tape and loose adhesive;
- staples;
- plastic film or wrapping;
- sharp cut edges;
- exposed wire;
- cracked plastic;
- rough or splintered wood;
- loose strings;
- fraying fabric when the dog mouths cloth.

With cardboard boxes, remove packaging material completely before the dog reaches the setup.

## 4. Swallow risk

Material safety depends partly on the dog.

One dog may sniff through paper and walk away. Another may immediately shred and ingest it. A towel may work as a loose search surface for one dog and become something another dog tears apart.

If the dog turns the material itself into the reward, remove the setup. Do not keep paying the dog with food while leaving an unsafe chewing or swallowing opportunity available.

A durable purpose-made puzzle, a room scatter, a toy search, a hand-based scent game, or another no-equipment activity may be the better choice.

## Supervise the setup while it is accessible

DIY enrichment is not an unsupervised storage solution.

Stay present while household materials are on the floor. If the dog chews, tears, swallows, becomes trapped, or starts using the setup in a way you did not intend, end the activity and remove the materials.

Supervision also lets you see when the challenge is wrong. Frantic grabbing, repeated escape attempts, escalating chewing, obvious fatigue, or signs of pain are reasons to simplify or stop.

## Treat food-contact items like feeding equipment

Bowls, trays, mats, durable toys, and fabrics that repeatedly hold food need a realistic cleaning routine.

Wash reusable items according to the material and manufacturer guidance. Wipe or wash fabric that repeatedly holds food. Discard damp, dirty, or damaged cardboard rather than reusing it because the setup took time to make.

A simple object you can clean properly is more useful than an elaborate puzzle that collects stale food.

## The safest substitute is often another activity

You do not need to redesign a questionable object until it becomes usable.

If a box, towel, cup, chair, tray, or other item fails one of the checks, switch activities. The dog still gets enrichment value from a safe alternative.
