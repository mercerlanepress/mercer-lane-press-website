The useful third-shot question is not **Which shot is always best?**

It is **Which option best improves our situation from this ball?**

After a conventional opening, the serving team is often deeper while the receiving team is moving toward or already established near the non-volley-zone line. That positional problem matters more than whether you personally prefer to drop or drive.

## Start with the job

The third shot usually needs to improve the serving team's positional situation.

A drop can do that by taking attacking leverage away and buying time to advance.

A drive can do it by forcing a weaker fourth ball that creates an easier fifth-shot decision.

A controlled roll or intermediate-paced ball may also have a role if it is an intentional, reliable option.

The stroke name is secondary to the job.

## Read four variables

### 1. Contact height

A lower ball generally makes a controlled drop more attractive.

A more comfortable or higher ball makes a drive more available.

"Available" does not mean automatic. A high ball hit to two balanced opponents may still produce a strong counter.

### 2. Your balance

A balanced player has options.

A stretched, late, or badly moving player should value control. Turning a difficult contact into a low-margin attack usually adds another problem rather than solving the first one.

### 3. Opponent position

If both opponents are balanced at the NVZ, driving directly through them may simply give them a comfortable block or counter.

If one opponent is late, moving, reaching, or leaving a large gap, a drive or firmer placement may create a more useful fourth ball.

### 4. Your actual reliability

A theoretically ideal shot is not your practical percentage option if you cannot execute it often enough to make the pattern work.

Use the shots you actually own in a match. Build the missing option in practice.

## Judge the drop by the opponent's contact

A drop is not successful merely because it lands in the kitchen.

Ask what the opponent can do with it.

Did the ball force a contact below net height?

Did it reduce attacking leverage?

Did it buy your team time?

Or did it sit high enough for a comfortable downward attack?

The opponent's options tell you more than the painted landing area.

## Judge the drive by the fourth ball

A drive is not successful merely because it was fast.

Ask what reply it is likely to produce.

A controlled block at your feet demands one plan.

A weak floating volley or short block may create a very different fifth shot.

If your drive creates a manageable next ball, it may have done its job even if it did not threaten to end the rally.

That is why the pattern can be:

**drive → weaker fourth → fifth-shot drop → advance**

rather than "drive until something wins."

## Do not sprint because the shot had the right name

Your partner's third-shot drop does not automatically authorize both players to charge.

Read the quality of the shot and the opponent's contact.

If the ball stays low and reduces attackability, take the ground it earned.

If it sits high, stop and prepare to defend.

The same principle applies after a drive. A strong drive that produces a weak block can create movement. A drive that comes straight back at your feet can take movement away.

## Treat the fifth shot as a new decision

The fifth shot is not an automatic continuation of the third-shot plan.

Read it again.

Where are you contacting?

What height did the fourth ball give you?

Are the opponents balanced or reaching?

Did the rally move from pressure toward neutral, or did a genuine attacking opportunity appear?

The serving team earns forward position through ball quality, not because the rally has reached a particular shot number.

The practical habit is simple:

**choose the third shot for the situation, watch what it creates, then reassess.**
