The most useful first week in a new home is usually not the week when you complete the most projects.

It is the week when you make the property **understandable**.

Before you repaint rooms, replace working fixtures, or build a long maintenance calendar, establish the controls, records, and baseline information you will need when something changes.

## First 48 hours: find the things you may need quickly

### 1. Locate the main water control

Find the main water control and record where it is.

Make sure another responsible adult in the household knows the location too.

Do not force an old, corroded, damaged, or unfamiliar valve simply to prove that it moves. If the control appears unreliable or inaccessible, put assessment on the action list instead.

Knowing where water can be controlled is far more useful before a leak than during one.

### 2. Locate the electrical panel

Know where the panel is and keep access clear.

You do not need to remove the cover or explore the internal equipment. The first-week job is identification and access.

If the panel area is wet, damaged, unusually hot, producing a burning odor, or showing another abnormal electrical condition, stop treating the task as routine setup and arrange appropriate help.

For a broader decision framework, see [DIY or call a professional?](/first-year-homeowner/diy-or-call-a-professional-home-repair/).

### 3. Identify normal heating and cooling controls

Find the thermostat and ordinary user controls.

Record what type of system you actually have: furnace, heat pump, boiler, central air conditioner, ductless equipment, electric resistance heat, room equipment, or another arrangement.

Find the manufacturer documentation where possible. Equipment-specific maintenance should come from the actual system rather than a generic internet calendar.

### 4. Review smoke and carbon-monoxide protection

Identify the alarms in the home and use their designed test functions according to current official and manufacturer guidance.

Record dates and any obvious missing, damaged, or expired equipment that needs attention.

The goal is not to improvise a test procedure. It is to know what safety equipment is present, how it is maintained, and what the household should do if an alarm activates.

### 5. Check obvious water-risk locations

Use a flashlight and look at accessible areas such as:

- under sinks;
- around toilets;
- the washing-machine connections;
- the dishwasher area;
- a refrigerator water connection where present;
- the water-heater area;
- ceilings beneath bathrooms;
- accessible utility, basement, or crawlspace areas.

You are looking for evidence: dampness, active dripping, staining, swelling, corrosion, or other visible change.

Do not dismantle fittings to investigate.

If you find moisture, use the [water and moisture warning-sign guide](/first-year-homeowner/water-and-moisture-warning-signs-homeowners/) to decide what information to record and when the issue needs faster attention.

### 6. Create an emergency contact note

Record the contacts you would want without searching under pressure. Depending on the property, that may include:

- emergency services;
- utility emergency numbers;
- insurance contact;
- plumber;
- electrician;
- HVAC professional;
- another relevant specialist.

You do not need to have a preferred contractor for every trade on day one. The useful part is creating a place for the information now.

### 7. Retrieve the purchase inspection

If you had a pre-purchase inspection, move it out of the buying-process folder and into your active home records.

Read it with a different question:

**What does this tell me about maintaining this property now that I own it?**

Extract significant repair recommendations, specialist evaluations, maintenance observations, inaccessible areas, conditions recommended for monitoring, and information about major systems.

## Days 3–7: give the house a memory

### Create a Home Record

Use a physical folder, cloud folder, local digital folder, or a combination you will actually maintain.

Keep equipment information, service history, warranties, photographs, contractor records, and observations somewhere predictable.

The [home-maintenance records system](/first-year-homeowner/home-maintenance-records-system/) gives you a simple structure.

### Photograph equipment labels

Capture manufacturer, model, serial number, and other useful identifiers for major equipment where safely visible.

These photographs can save time when you later need a manual, filter specification, service conversation, warranty claim, or replacement comparison.

### Take baseline photographs

Useful baseline views include:

- each exterior side;
- roof from safe ground-level viewpoints;
- visible drainage routes;
- utility areas;
- known cracks;
- known stains;
- concerns noted in the purchase inspection.

Repeatability matters more than photographic perfection. Future photographs become more useful when you can compare the same location from a similar angle.

### Sort inherited issues

Give every significant inherited issue a status.

**ACT** — it needs prompt action or assessment.

**SCHEDULE** — it can reasonably be planned.

**MONITOR** — observation is appropriate.

A genuine monitoring plan needs four things:

1. a specific condition;
2. a baseline;
3. a review date;
4. an escalation trigger.

“Keep an eye on the crack” is not yet a plan.

“Photograph this doorway crack today, compare it in three months, and escalate sooner if it clearly enlarges or appears with related movement” is much more useful.

## Your first Saturday: a short orientation walk

Take your phone, a flashlight, the inspection report, and your notes.

Walk the property in a repeatable order. A simple route might include:

1. utility area;
2. kitchen water connections;
3. bathrooms;
4. laundry;
5. safely accessible basement, attic access, or crawlspace entrance where applicable;
6. exterior perimeter;
7. electrical-panel location;
8. alarm locations.

Your output is not a complete repair list.

It is three answers:

- **What do I have?**
- **Where is it?**
- **What already needs attention?**

That is enough for the first week.

Next, turn this orientation into a short repeatable [monthly home-maintenance walk](/first-year-homeowner/monthly-home-maintenance-walk/).

The first-year system works best when the house becomes more specific and the checklist becomes shorter—not when every possible maintenance task is added forever.
