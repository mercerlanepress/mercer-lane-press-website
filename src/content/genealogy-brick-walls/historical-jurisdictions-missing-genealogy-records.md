A modern genealogy database makes place look simple. Historical record keeping was not.

The person lived in a physical place. Records about that person may have been created by several different civil, religious, judicial, military, or administrative authorities. Boundaries changed. Counties formed and divided. Courts had different responsibilities. Records moved to new custodians.

That means a place is not only a point on a map. For genealogy, it is also a record-creation system.

## Reconstruct the place at the relevant date

For the period you are researching, establish:

1. What country, territory, state, province, or region governed the place?
2. What county or equivalent existed on that date?
3. Was the town or settlement inside that jurisdiction then?
4. Did the boundary change during the research period?
5. What civil authorities created the records you want?
6. What religious jurisdictions or congregations may matter?
7. Where are those records held now?

If a county was created later from an older county, the ancestor may have stayed on the same farm while the jurisdiction changed around them. Earlier deeds, taxes, marriages, probate, or court records may be filed under the parent jurisdiction.

## Separate residence from record jurisdiction

A person can live in one place while the relevant record is created somewhere else.

A rural resident might own land recorded at a county courthouse, attend a church across a boundary, marry in a neighbouring jurisdiction, naturalize in a court that was convenient rather than geographically obvious, appear in a newspaper published in a nearby town, die in a hospital elsewhere, or be buried in another township.

Ask three questions for each desired record:

**Who created it?  
Who has custody now?  
Where can I access it?**

Those answers can be different.

## Do not confuse "not online" with "does not exist"

When a database search fails, separate these possibilities:

- the record series never existed;
- it existed but does not survive;
- it survives but is not online;
- it is online but not indexed;
- it is indexed incorrectly;
- the search strategy did not locate it.

Check collection coverage before interpreting absence. A collection can omit certain years, districts, volumes, or record types even when the title sounds broad.

## Use historical maps as research tools

Historical maps can help explain old county lines, neighbouring jurisdictions, roads, rivers, railways, nearby churches and cemeteries, settlements, and migration routes.

A neighbour half a mile away may have lived in another administrative area. The nearest church may have been across a county line. Distance and jurisdiction both matter.

## Substitute for the fact, not the missing document

When the ideal record truly does not survive, ask what fact you were hoping it would establish.

If the missing document is a marriage certificate, you may really need the spouse's identity, a date, a maiden name, parents, residence, previous marital status, or witnesses.

Different questions can have different substitutes. Depending on time and place, useful alternatives may include church registers, newspaper notices, children's records, probate, deeds, pension files, tax records, directories, guardianship, cemetery or burial material, and family-cluster evidence.

One missing record type does not automatically mean the fact is unknowable.

## Record what you actually checked

A useful locality note should include physical residence, jurisdiction at the beginning and end of the period, boundary changes, neighbouring jurisdictions, relevant record creators, current custodians, known record losses, collections searched, and collections not yet searched.

This turns "I cannot find it" into a specific statement about what has and has not been examined.
