A call-out changes the amount of work the department can realistically complete. The common mistake is to leave the original plan intact and simply divide the absent employee's rooms among everyone else.

That may make the paper look complete while making the actual boards impossible.

## Use a seven-step response

A useful sequence is:

**Freeze → Recount → Protect → Rebalance → Support → Communicate → Escalate**

### Freeze

Stop making casual additions to boards while you work out what changed.

You need a clear picture before redistributing rooms.

### Recount

Identify the capacity that disappeared.

Confirm:

- how many attendants are now available
- which support roles are present
- whether an inspector or supervisor is joining later
- what rooms were on the absent employee's board
- which rooms have not yet released
- which work can start now
- which rooms carry genuine timing or operational priority

The important question is not "Who can take this board?" It is "What work can the remaining capacity reasonably carry?"

### Protect

Identify what genuinely needs protection.

That can include a confirmed early-priority room, a specialist room, a room with a narrow operational window, or another requirement recognised by the property.

Do not convert the entire house into priority work. If everything is urgent, the word stops helping you make decisions.

### Rebalance

Redistribute work using the same factors that make a normal room board defensible:

- workload
- room type
- service type
- geography
- release timing
- experience
- existing board weight

Move work in both directions if necessary. If one board receives a more difficult room, simpler work may need to move out.

### Support

Check for authorised additional capacity.

That might include a trained employee from another area, an additional inspector arriving later, or another form of support allowed by the hotel's operating procedures.

Do not assume support exists until it is confirmed.

### Communicate

Tell the people affected what changed.

If rooms move between attendants, both the employee losing the rooms and the employee receiving them need accurate information. The working system should also reflect the change.

Front desk or management may need an updated operating picture if priorities or likely readiness have changed.

### Escalate

If the available capacity still cannot carry the work reasonably, identify the remaining gap clearly.

A useful escalation explains:

- what changed
- what has already been rebalanced
- which priorities have been protected
- what authorised support has been checked
- what work still does not fit the available capacity
- what decision or support is now needed

Do not hide missing capacity inside overloaded boards.

## Keep checking after the first replan

A call-out is not "solved" once the boards have been redistributed.

The bottleneck can move.

For example:

- the team may recover cleaning capacity but create a large inspection queue
- several late checkouts may release together
- a maintenance block may remove rooms from the normal flow
- a linen shortage may slow several boards
- a priority room may become more or less urgent as guest circumstances change

At each control point, ask:

**Where is the operation constrained now?**

## What not to do

Avoid these responses:

- divide the absent employee's rooms equally without checking total workload
- assume the fastest attendant can absorb the biggest share
- promise unchanged completion times before the new plan has been tested
- keep unreleased late-checkout rooms inside an already overloaded active board
- rebuild every board repeatedly when a smaller local adjustment would solve the new problem
- wait until the end of the shift to tell management that the remaining work never fitted the available capacity

## A concise call-out update

When another department needs an update, keep it factual.

State the staffing change, what you have protected, what you have rebalanced, the current bottleneck, and what still needs a decision.

That is more useful than saying the department is "doing its best," because it shows what the real operating problem is and what should happen next.
