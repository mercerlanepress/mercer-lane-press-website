A handover is not a summary of how busy the day felt.

It is an operating bridge between one shift and the next.

The test is simple:

**Can the next person continue the work without needing information that exists only in your memory?**

## Start with unresolved priorities

List the priorities that still matter.

For each one, make clear whether it is:

- resolved
- still in progress
- waiting for inspection
- blocked
- transferred to another owner
- dependent on a later action

Avoid leaving the next supervisor to rediscover which room was important and why.

## Record unfinished rooms by stage and next action

"Room 412 not done" is weak handover information.

A useful unfinished-room note explains:

- room number
- current stage
- reason it is unfinished
- next action
- who owns that action
- when or what should trigger the recheck

This is especially important for rooms that are clean except for one unresolved issue.

## Treat blocked rooms as controlled exceptions

Every blocked room should have a next action and an owner.

For a maintenance problem, describe the observable condition rather than diagnosing beyond your training.

The handover should make clear:

- what housekeeping has completed
- what remains blocked
- whether maintenance or another department has the issue
- whether front desk has been informed where appropriate
- what housekeeping must do after the room is released

A blocked room without a next action is easy to forget.

## Carry forward linen, supply, and equipment risk

Small supply problems can become large delays on the next shift.

Record items that may affect room completion, including:

- linen running low in a particular area
- stock that exists but has not reached the floor where it is needed
- equipment that has failed or been removed from service
- replenishment already requested
- the person or department expected to act
- the next check point

Distinguish a total shortage from a distribution problem. The next supervisor needs to know whether stock does not exist or simply is not where the work is happening.

## Flag room-status or front-desk information that still needs checking

If the physical room and system status do not match, hand it over.

If front desk is waiting for an update, record it.

If a late checkout, extension, room move, or maintenance release may change the next operating picture, make that visible.

The handover should prevent the next shift from starting with stale information.

## Record staff or training follow-up appropriately

A shift may end before a coaching follow-up is complete.

Record enough for the authorised next step, using the employer's normal process and appropriate confidentiality.

Do not turn sensitive employee information into casual handover notes. Use the records and routes required by the property.

## Give the next supervisor a first action

One of the most useful handover fields is:

**First action for the next supervisor**

That can be a reinspection, a maintenance recheck, a front-desk update, a linen follow-up, or another clear priority.

It turns the handover from a list of facts into a usable starting point.

## A compact handover checklist

Before leaving, confirm that the handover covers:

- priorities resolved and outstanding
- unfinished rooms with current stage and next action
- blocked rooms with owner and recheck
- maintenance or out-of-service issues
- linen, supply, and equipment risks
- room-status or front-desk information still to verify
- employee or training follow-up recorded through the appropriate route
- the next supervisor's first action

The shift does not need to end with every problem solved.

It does need to end with unresolved work visible enough for someone else to continue safely and deliberately.
