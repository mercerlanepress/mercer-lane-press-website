A folder of records tells you what you have found. A timeline tells you what the evidence says happened.

For a genealogy brick wall, that difference matters. Records viewed one at a time can make contradictions and missing periods easy to overlook. Put them into one chronological sequence and the case often changes shape.

## Start with events that affect the research question

Do not try to transcribe the person's entire life history. Begin with events that help answer the current question:

- births and baptisms;
- marriages and deaths;
- census appearances;
- city directories;
- land, tax, probate, court, or military events;
- immigration and naturalization;
- church membership;
- children's births;
- known residences and occupations;
- appearances as a witness, sponsor, or informant.

Include a spouse, sibling, child, or associate when that person's event helps establish the target's location or identity.

## Preserve what the source actually says

Suppose your family-tree software displays:

**Birth: 2 March 1852, Boston, Massachusetts**

But the evidence behind it is:

- 1880 census: age 30, born Massachusetts;
- 1900 census: born March 1852, Massachusetts;
- death certificate: born 2 March 1850, Boston.

Do not collapse those statements into one perfect date. Put each record into the timeline as it stands.

Useful columns are:

| Date or range | Person and event | Place | Source and key information | Confidence, conflict, or lead |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1880 | Census | Lowell, Massachusetts | Age 30; birthplace Massachusetts | Birth year approximate |
| 1900 | Census | Boston, Massachusetts | Born Mar 1852 | More specific but still one report |
| 1918 | Death | Boston, Massachusetts | Born 2 Mar 1850 | Conflicts with 1900 |

The timeline is not the place to force agreement. It is the place to make disagreement visible.

## Use ranges when the evidence uses ranges

Historical records often support an interval rather than an exact date.

If a child appears in an 1870 census aged four, that does not create an exact birth date. If a family is in Ohio in 1860 and Kansas in 1870, the move occurred somewhere between those appearances unless another source narrows it.

Approximation is useful. False precision can make later searches unnecessarily rigid.

## Look for impossible sequences

Once the timeline exists, inspect it as though somebody else built it.

Ask:

- Is the same person apparently in two distant places at nearly the same time?
- Does a child appear before the supposed parents' chronology makes sense?
- Does a person appear in records after an alleged death?
- Does the spouse or set of children change abruptly?
- Does an occupation, residence, or birthplace shift together with other identity details?

One odd detail may be ordinary reporting noise. Several changes together may be an identity warning.

## Find the empty periods

A timeline is especially valuable for turning "I cannot find him" into a bounded interval.

For example:

- 1872 — married in Pennsylvania
- 1874 — first child born in Pennsylvania
- 1876 — second child born in Pennsylvania
- 1880 — family in Missouri
- 1900 — widow living in Colorado

Now you have narrower questions:

- When did the family move between 1876 and 1880?
- What happened to the husband between 1880 and 1900?
- When did the widow move to Colorado?

Those questions suggest different record sets and different searches.

## Track place at the level the source provides

Record the street, town, township, county, state, country, parish, district, or other jurisdiction when the source gives it.

If the family appears to jump geographically, do not immediately assume a move. The boundary may have moved, the same location may have been described by different jurisdictions, or two people may have been combined.

That is the point where the [historical-jurisdictions guide](/genealogy-brick-walls/historical-jurisdictions-missing-genealogy-records/) becomes useful.

## Finish by marking the next test

A completed timeline should make at least one of these visible:

- a gap;
- a contradiction;
- a transition;
- a cluster of associates;
- an unsupported assumption.

Do not respond automatically with another broad name search. Choose the next action because it addresses something the timeline exposed.

If the timeline contains several incompatible versions of the same fact, use a [contradiction matrix](/genealogy-brick-walls/resolve-conflicting-genealogy-records/) before deciding which version belongs in the tree.
