A manufacturing shift rarely follows the first plan exactly.

Someone is absent. Material arrives late. A machine stops. Quality needs a hold checked. Planning changes the priority. A team member needs help. Your manager asks for an update.

That is why a new supervisor needs an operating rhythm rather than a perfect timetable.

Use:

**Prepare → Brief → Observe → Respond → Follow Up → Handover → Review**

The sequence is useful because it gives you a way to reset when the assumptions behind the shift change.

## Before the shift: prepare the picture

Use a five-minute check across five areas.

### People

Ask:

- Who is here?
- Who is absent?
- Does absence create a competence or training problem?
- Is anyone new, training, restricted to certain work, or otherwise operating within limits set by your organisation?

### Work

Ask:

- What is today’s main priority?
- What is already in progress?
- What is unfinished from the previous shift?
- What deadlines or customer requirements matter today?

### Problems

Check for known:

- breakdowns;
- quality holds;
- missing material;
- staffing shortages;
- blocked jobs;
- previous-shift issues.

### Changes

Identify any:

- schedule change;
- new instruction;
- process change;
- staffing change;
- altered production priority.

Make sure a change has been communicated or trained through the appropriate organisational route rather than assuming an email alone means everyone understands it.

### Escalation

Ask whether anything needs another person’s decision before you can organise the shift sensibly.

If two priorities cannot both be met with the available people, the best supervisory action may be to make the trade-off visible and ask the person with authority to choose.

## Brief the team with information they can act on

A useful shift briefing is short and operational.

Cover:

- the main priority;
- significant staffing or allocation changes;
- important carryover;
- relevant quality or process information;
- relevant safety information through established site arrangements;
- one thing people must understand before starting;
- questions or confirmation needed.

Avoid turning the briefing into a long speech.

The team needs enough information to understand the current plan, not a complete retelling of everything you know.

## Observe without hovering

Supervision requires visibility, but visibility is not the same as standing over competent employees.

Useful questions include:

- What is most likely to stop this run?
- Has anything changed since the briefing?
- Where are you against the plan?
- What decision do you need from me?

These questions produce more information than a repeated “Everything okay?”

Spend attention according to legitimate need.

A stable, experienced area may need less intervention than a new operator, a newly started line, or an area recovering from disruption. Fair supervision does not require identical minutes in every part of the shift.

## Respond using the right problem category

Before blaming the nearest person, ask whether the issue is primarily:

- **People** — a person’s action, decision, conduct, or performance;
- **Work** — staffing, material, equipment, process, workload, or another operational constraint;
- **Clarity** — unclear or inconsistently communicated expectations;
- **Authority** — a decision requiring somebody else.

Several categories can apply at once.

The purpose of the diagnostic is to stop a supervisor deciding that the employee is the problem before establishing what actually happened.

## Keep follow-up visible

A large part of supervisory credibility is reliable follow-up.

If you tell an employee you will check training, ask Planning a question, clarify a quality route, or find out who owns an issue, capture the action immediately.

Use one appropriate follow-up system.

Do not let a private notebook become an unofficial personnel file. Record the action you need to complete rather than unnecessary sensitive information about employees.

## Build a useful end-of-shift handover

A useful handover answers:

- What is the current state?
- What remains unfinished?
- What changed?
- What has already been arranged?
- What may become a problem next?
- What does the incoming supervisor actually need to know?

The goal is not to retell the entire shift.

A good handover distinguishes between:

- a job delayed because the team failed to act;
- a job delayed because equipment is still under Maintenance control;
- a product waiting for Quality;
- a priority deliberately deferred by management;
- an action already assigned to someone.

That context helps the next shift act rather than investigate the same history again.

## End-of-shift closeout checklist

Before leaving, check:

- unfinished work identified;
- next-shift handover prepared;
- employee follow-up captured;
- unresolved quality issues routed correctly;
- safety matters requiring site action handled through the proper process;
- open commitments recorded;
- manager questions or escalations identified;
- one thing you did personally that could be delegated next time.

This last question matters.

A supervisor can finish an apparently successful shift while quietly training the team to depend on them for every difficult task.

## Review the week, not just the shift

Once a week, spend a short period reviewing:

**People** — Who needs feedback, recognition, clarification, follow-up, or development?

**Repetition** — What problem appeared more than once?

**Delegation** — What did I personally do that someone else could reasonably own?

**Commitments** — What am I late doing?

**Managing up** — What issue does my manager need to know about?

**Avoidance** — What conversation am I hoping disappears?

**Next week** — What are the three most useful supervisory priorities?

This turns a chaotic sequence of shifts into a manageable next week.

## When the shift is disrupted, restart the cycle

Suppose the shift begins normally and then:

- an employee reports sick;
- a line expected to restart remains down;
- Planning changes the priority.

Do not keep managing against the original assumptions.

Prepare again.

Brief the people affected.

Observe the areas newly exposed by the change.

Respond to the actual constraint.

Follow up decisions.

Hand over what remains unresolved.

The operating rhythm is valuable precisely because the timetable has failed.

## A handover is also a management boundary

When an unresolved issue belongs to Quality, Maintenance, Engineering, HR, Safety, a manager, or another specialist, say so clearly.

Do not create the impression that the incoming shift should solve something it does not own.
