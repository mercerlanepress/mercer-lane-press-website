Two paddles move toward the middle.

Then both stop.

On the next rally, both players swing and nearly collide.

The middle is often less a reflex problem than a responsibility problem.

## Stop treating the center line as a permanent wall

The center line matters for the service courts.

Once the rally is live, the two partners have to cover the available space as a team.

That means "your half" and "my half" are only starting simplifications.

A ball can arrive where responsibilities overlap.

Partner position, handedness, movement, contact quality, and prior agreement all matter.

## Rule 1: agree a default before play

A default removes hesitation when both players are equally well positioned.

For many righty-righty partnerships, the forehand toward the middle may receive a preference.

Another team may decide that one player owns neutral middle balls.

The exact default is less important than both players understanding it.

A default is not absolute ownership.

## Rule 2: let position override theory

Suppose the "forehand takes middle" player is stretched, late, or moving away from the ball while the partner is balanced and positioned cleanly behind it.

Rigidly enforcing the forehand rule makes the team worse.

The player with the clearly better contact should usually take the ball.

Formation changes responsibility.

## Rule 3: call early enough to matter

Simple calls work:

**Me.**

**You.**

The useful call is early enough to change the decision.

Communication after both paddles have already committed is not communication; it is commentary.

## Rule 4: the hitter owns recovery too

Taking a middle ball can move a player across the team's normal spacing.

The decision is not complete at contact.

After the shot, recover into a relationship that can cover the likely reply.

This is especially important after an aggressive poach or speed-up through the middle.

## Rule 5: review repeated conflicts between rallies

Do not negotiate while the ball is coming.

If the same uncertainty keeps appearing, adjust the default between points.

Ask what type of ball caused the conflict:

- high attackable middle ball;
- low neutral dink;
- body ball;
- ball behind the pair;
- ball created after one partner moved wide.

A useful middle system can include different responsibilities for different situations.

## Righty-lefty pairs still need a rule

A right-handed player on the left and a left-handed player on the right can put two forehands toward the center.

That can be useful.

It can also create a collision if both players believe "forehand takes middle" settles the question.

Two forehands make agreement more important, not less.

The team still needs a default and an override for the player with the better position.

## Responsibility follows movement

If your partner is pulled wide, you normally protect more of the newly opened middle.

If you cross to take an attack, your partner's realistic coverage changes.

If one player is trapped deep, middle responsibility changes again.

That is why [partner positioning and connected coverage](/pickleball-doubles/pickleball-doubles-partner-positioning/) matter more than permanent court halves.

A simple pre-game middle plan can be:

1. agree the neutral default;
2. let better position override it;
3. call early when ownership is unclear;
4. recover after taking the ball;
5. adjust the system if the same conflict repeats.

The middle belongs to the team. The responsibility has to be clear before the ball arrives.
