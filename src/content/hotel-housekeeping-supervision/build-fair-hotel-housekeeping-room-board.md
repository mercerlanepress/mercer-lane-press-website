A room board can look equal and still be operationally unfair. Ten rooms on one board are not automatically equivalent to ten rooms on another.

A supervisor needs a way to explain an allocation using visible work factors rather than habit, speed, or whoever is least likely to complain.

## Start with the house, not the headcount

Before assigning rooms, confirm the work that actually exists:

- departures
- stayovers or service rooms
- vacant dirty rooms
- late checkouts
- rooms already ready
- unavailable or out-of-service rooms
- genuine priority rooms
- known maintenance holds
- unusual room types or authorised setups

Then confirm the people who actually arrived. A schedule is a staffing plan, not proof of available capacity.

If the property already uses an approved credits, points, minutes, or productivity system, use that system. Do not replace it with an unofficial formula.

## Use six checks before you call a board fair

A practical fairness check looks at six things.

### 1. Count

Does one person simply have materially more work than the others?

Raw count is useful as a first warning, but it is only the first check.

### 2. Workload

Do room types or service types make the count misleading?

A departure, stayover, suite, multiple-bed room, or room with an authorised additional setup may carry different work. Use the property's own workload method where one exists.

### 3. Distance

Is the board geographically sensible?

A board spread across distant floors, wings, an annex, or awkward routes can lose time even if the room total looks balanced. Geography matters because the employee has to move between rooms, carts, linen, elevators, and service areas.

### 4. Timing

Does one board contain several late checkouts or genuine priority arrivals?

A late checkout changes when work can start. A priority room can change the order of work. Do not keep adding work to a board merely because its current numerical total looks lighter.

### 5. Experience

Does training level matter?

A new employee may reasonably need a manageable assignment and more checking. A trained employee may be suitable for a specialist room type. The point is to use a legitimate work reason, not assumptions about the person.

### 6. Pattern

Is the same strong employee repeatedly absorbing disruption?

Competence can quietly become a penalty. If the same reliable attendant always receives the extra departure, difficult room, or emergency priority, review the pattern across several shifts.

## A practical board-building sequence

Use this order:

1. **Map the work.** Confirm room types, service types, late checkouts, priorities, and blocked rooms.
2. **Confirm actual staffing.** Build around people present and authorised support actually available.
3. **Draft geographically.** Keep floors, wings, and travel sensible where possible.
4. **Check workload.** Compare the real weight of the boards rather than room count alone.
5. **Check timing.** Protect boards already carrying late releases or genuine priorities.
6. **Check experience.** Make sure the assignment fits training needs and authorised roles.
7. **Check the pattern.** Ask whether the same person is repeatedly carrying disruption.
8. **Issue the board and brief exceptions.** Employees should know what is unusual today.
9. **Recheck after the shift starts.** A fair 8:00 a.m. plan can become unfair after call-outs, room moves, late releases, or maintenance blocks.

## When an attendant says the board is unfair

Do not reject the complaint automatically, and do not accept it automatically.

Ask the employee to show what makes the board heavier. That turns the conversation toward observable work: suites, distance, late checkouts, priorities, or another real constraint.

If the employee has identified something you missed, correct the board. If the allocation remains reasonable, explain the operational reason.

Changing a board because the evidence changed is not a loss of authority. It is part of supervising the work.

## Common mistakes

Avoid these patterns:

- giving everyone the same room count without checking room type or service type
- treating every early-arrival request as a priority
- loading extra work onto the fastest employee whenever the plan breaks
- ignoring geography because the totals look balanced
- preserving the original board after the available capacity has changed
- making repeated changes without updating the working system or telling everyone affected

## A final question before boards go out

Ask:

**Can I explain why this board is reasonable using the work that exists today?**

If the answer depends mainly on "that is how we always do it" or "they are faster," the board deserves another look.
