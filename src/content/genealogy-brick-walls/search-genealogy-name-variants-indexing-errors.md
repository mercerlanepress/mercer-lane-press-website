A neatly standardized name in a family tree can create the impression that the person should appear under that same form everywhere. Historical records do not work that way.

One person might appear as Johann Muller, John Miller, Jno. Miller, Johannes Mueller, J. Miller, or under an indexing error that does not resemble any form you expected.

The useful question is: **How might this person's name have been written, heard, translated, abbreviated, remembered, or indexed?**

## Start with variants the evidence already gives you

Before inventing alternatives, collect every observed form from records you have actually examined.

For given names, note formal forms, shortened forms, nicknames, initials, translated or Anglicised forms, and a middle name used as the everyday first name.

For surnames, note observed and phonetic spellings, spacing and prefix differences, simplified or earlier-language forms, stepfamily or alternate surnames, married and previous married surnames, and obvious transcription possibilities.

Do not generate fifty random spellings. Build a small set with a reason behind each variation.

## Inspect the underlying image where possible

An index is a discovery tool. It is not automatically a faithful copy of the source.

If the indexed surname looks wrong, inspect the image. The transcriber was solving the same handwriting problem you are.

Also remember the copy chain. A modern database may inherit an error from an older transcription, printed index, or abstract. Seeing the same spelling on several websites does not prove that the underlying sources independently used that spelling.

## Search without the surname

When the surname itself is the problem, stop requiring it.

Suppose you are looking for Michael Szczepanski in Chicago around 1910 with wife Helena and children Joseph and Sophia. Useful alternatives can include Michael and Helena in the right place, Helena and Joseph, a child with a rarer given name, occupation plus approximate age, street or neighbourhood, or another household member whose name was indexed more clearly.

The database may have mangled the surname beyond anything you would predict.

## Search the household, not just the target

A badly indexed parent can sit inside a household where a child is indexed correctly.

For a family cluster, ask: **Which person gives the search engine the best chance of finding the household?**

A child with an unusual first name, a spouse with a more stable surname, or a repeated address can be more useful than the target ancestor.

## Build a surname chronology

Women can be especially easy to lose when research follows only one surname.

Put every observed surname in date order. A sequence such as Anna Keller at baptism, Anna Vogel on a child's record, Anna Vogel on a marriage record describing her as a widow, and Anna Schmidt in a later census may reveal a previous marriage rather than a clerical error.

The same principle can apply to stepchildren, alternate surnames, translated names, and other identity changes.

## Loosen one field at a time

When an index keeps failing, change the search systematically:

1. surname only;
2. given name plus place;
3. spouse plus place;
4. child plus place;
5. occupation plus approximate age;
6. browse the original collection where practical.

Search flexibility is not randomness. Keep a log of what changed and what result it produced.

## Identity still has to be proved

Finding a plausible variant is only the start. A name match does not establish identity.

Correlate the candidate with spouse, children, occupation, residence, associates, age progression, religion, property, and migration pattern. If several details change together, use the approach in [conflicting genealogy records](/genealogy-brick-walls/resolve-conflicting-genealogy-records/) to test whether the supposed "variant" actually belongs to a different person.
