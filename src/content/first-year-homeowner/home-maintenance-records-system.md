A useful Home Record gives the property a memory.

Without one, routine questions become surprisingly difficult:

- Was that crack always there?
- When was the HVAC system last serviced?
- Which filter did I buy?
- Who repaired the leak?
- Was this stain present at purchase?
- When does the warranty expire?
- What did the contractor tell me to watch afterward?

The answer does not require complicated software.

It requires one place you will continue to use.

## Choose a storage system you can maintain

A physical binder, local computer folder, cloud folder, or a hybrid can all work.

The best system is the one you can find during an ordinary service call and update after the work is finished.

A practical folder structure might include:

1. Property Overview
2. Purchase & Inspection
3. Water & Plumbing
4. Heating / Cooling / Ventilation
5. Electrical & Life Safety
6. Roof & Exterior
7. Appliances
8. Warranties
9. Contractors & Service
10. Observations
11. Photographs

Use fewer folders if that makes the system easier to maintain.

Do not build an elaborate filing system that becomes another abandoned home project.

## Start with a property overview

Create a one-page map of the systems you actually have.

Record:

- main water source and control;
- sewer, septic, or other wastewater arrangement;
- roof, gutter, downspout, and site-drainage notes;
- heating system;
- cooling system;
- bathroom and kitchen ventilation;
- dryer exhaust;
- electrical-panel location;
- smoke and CO alarm locations where applicable;
- household escape routes and meeting point;
- special systems such as a sump, generator, solar, battery storage, irrigation, radon mitigation, water treatment, pool, security equipment, or fireplace/chimney.

This prevents a generic maintenance list from filling your calendar with equipment you do not own.

## Build an equipment register once

For each important installed system or appliance, capture:

- equipment name;
- manufacturer;
- model;
- serial number;
- installation date if known;
- manual location;
- warranty information;
- service information.

A clear photograph of the equipment label can be useful, provided the label is safely accessible.

The register becomes the starting point for future manufacturer guidance and service conversations.

## Take baseline photographs before memory changes

Photographs become much more useful when they are repeatable.

For a known crack, stain, exterior condition, or drainage area:

- use a similar viewpoint each time;
- include a scale reference where useful;
- date the photograph;
- record the location;
- keep the image with the observation note.

The point is not to diagnose from a photograph.

It is to answer a narrower question later:

**Did this condition change?**

That distinction matters.

Use the [monthly maintenance walk](/first-year-homeowner/monthly-home-maintenance-walk/) to create the repeatable routine.

## Make service-history entries specific

“HVAC done” is not a strong record.

A useful service entry includes:

- date;
- system;
- company;
- work performed;
- findings;
- recommended follow-up;
- warranty or invoice location.

The record becomes valuable months or years later when a new technician asks what changed, what was replaced, or what the previous visit concluded.

## Keep warranties as an active register

Record:

- item;
- provider;
- start date;
- expiry date;
- relevant maintenance conditions;
- claim route;
- required documents.

Do not wait for a failure to discover that a warranty required maintenance records you never kept.

A quick annual review of the register can also stop expiring coverage from disappearing into paperwork.

## Keep a contractor contact log

For each company or professional you use, note:

- trade;
- company;
- credentials checked where relevant;
- work performed;
- warranty;
- whether you would use them again.

For significant work, preserve the written scope, invoice, relevant photographs, permit information where applicable, and any instructions about what to monitor afterward.

When comparing future proposals, compare the **scope** as well as the total price.

## Use an observation log for uncertain conditions

Not every observation is a repair.

Your log can be simple:

**Date | Location | What I noticed | Photo | Change? | Next action**

The important part is the next action.

An item should not sit forever as “watch this.”

A real monitoring entry contains:

1. a defined condition;
2. a baseline;
3. a review date;
4. an escalation trigger.

For example, a stable cosmetic crack might remain a monitoring item when appropriate. Clear progression, displacement, nearby movement, or another relevant change could move it to professional assessment.

The record does not make the diagnosis. It makes the decision process visible.

## Separate service, repair, emergency, and improvement spending

At the end of the first year, review what the property actually cost to operate.

Separate:

- routine maintenance;
- professional service;
- repairs;
- emergencies;
- improvements.

This gives you property-specific evidence rather than forcing every house into a generic percentage rule.

## Close records when the question is finished

A good Home Record is not an archive of permanent worry.

At review time, classify each monitored observation:

**Closed** — resolved or no longer meaningful.

**Stable — continue monitoring** — no material change, but there is still a reason to review it.

**Escalated** — change now justifies qualified assessment.

**Remove** — the item no longer deserves active tracking.

Likewise, mark material purchase-inspection items as resolved, professionally evaluated, stable, scheduled, or superseded by later information.

## Let the record make Year Two shorter

By the end of the first year, the useful maintenance calendar should be more specific than the generic list you started with.

Keep recurring tasks only when you can explain why they apply:

- the manufacturer requires it;
- a qualified professional recommended it;
- the local climate makes it relevant;
- the property repeatedly shows a condition that deserves attention.

If you cannot explain why a reminder is there, review whether it belongs on the calendar.

Start the record during your [first week as a homeowner](/first-year-homeowner/first-week-homeowner-checklist/), then let the evidence—not the size of the checklist—shape the maintenance system.
