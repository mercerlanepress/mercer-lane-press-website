You test the pool.

Chlorine is low.

You correct it.

Later it is low again.

After a few cycles it is tempting to say:

**My pool will not hold chlorine.**

That describes the pattern. The next job is to diagnose it.

## Some chlorine loss is normal

Outdoor free chlorine is consumed over time.

Sunlight and contaminants introduced by swimmers consume available chlorine, and cyanuric acid is commonly used in outdoor pools to reduce ultraviolet degradation.

The useful question is whether the loss is:

- expected;
- unusually rapid;
- persistent;
- connected to green or cloudy water;
- linked to a change in sanitizer output or pool use.

## Step 1: verify the pattern

Record more than the low number.

Useful fields include:

- date and time;
- free chlorine;
- pH;
- sun and weather conditions;
- swimmer use;
- product added or generator output;
- pump and sanitizer-system operation;
- water appearance.

One isolated low result carries less information than a repeatable pattern.

## Step 2: confirm a surprising test

If the result contradicts everything else you expect, repeat the test according to its instructions.

Check whether test materials are in date and stored correctly, whether the sample was taken correctly, and whether the reading method was followed.

A surprising result may deserve independent confirmation before a large correction.

The right response to an uncertain measurement can be **verify the test**, not **dose the pool immediately**.

## Step 3: confirm chlorine is actually being supplied

For manually chlorinated pools, confirm:

- the exact product;
- active ingredient;
- concentration;
- quantity;
- pool-volume assumption;
- application method;
- label instructions.

For a saltwater chlorine-generation system, remember that the pool still depends on chlorine.

Check only the owner-accessible information specified by the system's manual, such as:

- operating status;
- error messages;
- output setting;
- run schedule;
- salt indication;
- required flow state.

A controller that appears to be operating does not replace a water test.

## Step 4: look for increased demand

Consider:

- algae or early loss of clarity;
- heavy swimmer use;
- storms;
- leaves or other contamination;
- a period of inadequate sanitation;
- unusually warm or sunny conditions;
- contaminated water entering the pool.

If chlorine loss appears together with green or cloudy water, the symptoms are probably connected rather than separate problems.

Use the [green-water guide](/pool-troubleshooting/green-pool-algae-or-metals/) or [cloudy-water checklist](/pool-troubleshooting/cloudy-pool-water-troubleshooting/) alongside the chlorine-loss log.

## Step 5: consider stabilizer context

Know whether the pool contains cyanuric acid and whether stabilized chlorine products are used routinely.

Do not keep adding stabilized products without understanding that they can also affect stabilizer levels.

Stabilizer context changes how chlorine behavior is interpreted, but it does not remove the need to diagnose sanitizer supply, demand, and circulation.

## Day loss and overnight loss are different clues

Imagine a pool with clear water and normal circulation.

Free chlorine is reasonable late in the evening but much lower the following sunny afternoon.

That pattern makes sunlight and stabilizer context relevant.

Now change the pattern: chlorine falls rapidly overnight while sunlight is absent and the water begins to cloud.

Ordinary sunlight becomes a much weaker explanation, and biological or contaminant demand becomes more important.

The useful information is not simply **chlorine fell**.

It is **when, how quickly, and under what operating conditions it fell**.

## Change one justified thing and remeasure

Once the likely cause is isolated, correct it according to the relevant product label, test-system instructions, equipment manual, and measured pool condition.

Then retest.

Do not define success as "chlorine rose immediately after I added something."

The issue is resolved when the pool maintains its expected sanitizer condition under comparable operating conditions without repeated unexplained loss.

After a storm, use the [post-storm checklist](/pool-troubleshooting/pool-after-storm-checklist/) to reconnect sanitizer loss with debris, power interruption, water-level changes, circulation, and equipment condition.
