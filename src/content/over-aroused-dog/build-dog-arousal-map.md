When one day is manageable and the next feels chaotic, it is easy to keep changing the training plan.

Before changing the plan, improve the information.

An **arousal map** is a short record of the situations that raise or lower activation. It does not need to become a diary of every minute. It needs enough detail to answer four questions:

1. What predicts the problem?
2. What intensifies it?
3. What helps the dog recover?
4. What is changing over time?

## Record events, not labels

For each important event, write down:

- **Trigger or context:** what was happening?
- **Starting state:** resting, normal, already excited, already worried?
- **Distance or intensity:** how close, loud, long, fast, or difficult?
- **Behavior:** what did the dog physically do?
- **Response:** what did you do?
- **Recovery:** how long until the dog returned to a workable state?
- **Notes:** unusual sleep, exercise, visitors, illness, weather, travel, or other load.

Keep the language observable.

Instead of “terrible around visitors,” record something like: “Doorbell rang. Dog ran to gate, barked continuously, initially ignored food, then paced after the guest sat.”

That gives you variables you can change.

## Start with controllable triggers

Some events are hard to control: a loose dog appears around a corner or an unexpected delivery happens.

Other triggers are much easier to change:

- when and where the leash appears;
- whether a front blind stays open;
- how long ball play lasts;
- where the dog rests;
- how visitors enter;
- where food is prepared;
- whether greetings happen immediately.

Start with those “easy win” situations.

If the dog spins when the leash appears, handle the leash at quiet times when no walk follows. If the dog rehearses window barking many times a day, reduce visual access while you teach a different response.

Management is useful because it reduces repeated practice of the pattern you are trying to change.

## Test recovery activities instead of assuming

An activity marketed as calming is not automatically calming for your dog.

After moderate excitement, try one low-intensity option at a time and observe the effect. Possibilities include:

- unhurried sniffing in a quiet area;
- a small food search;
- a safe chew or food toy if appropriate;
- quiet contact if the dog seeks it;
- resting behind a barrier;
- a low-distraction wander in a suitable area;
- being left undisturbed in a comfortable place.

If a puzzle creates frustration or a chew creates conflict, it is not serving the intended purpose for that individual dog.

## Review the previous 24 hours after a big reaction

The final trigger may not explain the size of the reaction.

After a difficult event, look back over the previous day:

- Was sleep normal?
- Was exercise unusually intense or unusually limited?
- Were there visitors, travel, grooming, daycare, classes, storms, construction, or veterinary care?
- Did several smaller trigger events happen earlier?
- Did the dog have a chance to recover after them?
- Did appetite, toileting, movement, or comfort look different?

This is a practical way to look for **trigger stacking** without pretending that a numerical “stress bucket” score can diagnose the dog.

## Use measures that can actually change

“Calm all day” is too vague to guide training.

Better measures include:

- recovery after the doorbell becomes shorter;
- the dog can notice another dog at a workable distance and then disengage;
- mat duration increases while ordinary household movement happens;
- evening jumping or mouthiness becomes less frequent;
- the dog can end play and shift into sniffing without grabbing clothing;
- the dog can eat normally in a location that previously caused food refusal.

Look for trends across weeks. Progress is often uneven.

## Run a weekly review

At the end of the week, answer five questions:

1. Which three situations created the highest activation?
2. Which situations produced the easiest successful training?
3. Which recovery activity worked best?
4. Which management change prevented the most repeated unwanted behavior?
5. What single difficulty should change next week?

Do not overhaul everything at once. A useful map should make the next decision clearer, not create more moving parts.
