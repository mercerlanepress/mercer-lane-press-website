Food is common in dog enrichment because it is easy to hide, scatter, and divide into small rewards. It is not the only way to create a useful activity.

A no-food session can use a familiar toy, a person, an indoor route, a safe view, or a change in texture. The important part is that the activity gives the dog a safe opportunity to investigate, choose, search, move, play, or interact.

## Search for a toy

**Toy Search** starts with a toy the dog already likes.

Show the toy, play briefly, then place it partly behind a chair while the dog watches. Use a consistent search cue and let finding the toy lead directly into a short play burst.

Once the dog understands the idea, the toy can be hidden while the dog waits outside the room. Keep hiding places accessible; do not ask the dog to climb, squeeze behind furniture, or knock objects over.

A related option is **Where Did the Toy Go?** Cover only part of a familiar toy with a loose towel edge, then increase how much is concealed over several sessions if the dog stays comfortable with the material.

## Search for a person

**Find the Person** uses a scent the dog already knows.

One person stays with the dog while another hides partly behind a doorway and calls once. Later hides can become quieter or less visible, but the game should remain easy enough that the dog does not become worried.

For dogs that already recognize family members by name, **Family Name Search** can add a memory component. Start with the named person visible, then gradually move them just out of sight.

## Use familiar toys differently

Novelty does not require buying another toy.

With **Toy Rotation Reveal**, store some familiar toys out of sight between sessions and bring one back as the special item for the day. Inspect it before use and discard it if damaged.

**Three-Item Choice** offers several familiar toys with different textures and lets the dog choose which one starts the interaction. There is no correct answer. The choice itself gives you information about what the dog wants that day.

**Tug With Pauses** and **Roll-and-Return** can also be food-free. Keep tug low and controlled, and roll toys along the floor rather than turning indoor play into repeated high-speed fetching.

## Slow the movement down

Indoor enrichment does not need to be vigorous.

**Slow Follow** uses a clear room and deliberate walking with broad direction changes and pauses. **Copy My Route** adds two or three safe landmarks such as a mat, chair, or doorway. **Household Landmark Loop** turns familiar locations into a short walking sequence without jumps or tunnels.

Floors should be non-slip, routes clear, and turns broad. These are enrichment activities, not exercise prescriptions.

## Add observation, scent, or texture

Some dogs can enjoy **Window Watch With a Job** from a mat positioned back from a quiet view. This is only appropriate when watching does not increase barking, pacing, or distress.

**Scent Walk Indoors** simply changes the usual rule of moving efficiently through the house. Walk slowly through one or two checked rooms and allow the dog to pause at safe scent points.

**Texture Trail** uses flat, non-slip surfaces such as a towel, rubber-backed mat, and low-pile rug. Let the dog step onto them voluntarily. The goal is sensory variety, not unstable balance work.

## Let the dog decline

A dog that sniffs once and walks away has provided useful information.

The timing may be poor, the activity may be too difficult, or the dog may simply prefer another form of engagement. Simplify or choose something else rather than turning every refusal into a training task.
