A “ten-minute report audit” is a structure, not a promise that every home inspection report can be reviewed in exactly ten minutes.

Complex reports may require longer.

The useful idea is to replace an aimless top-to-bottom reread with targeted passes. Each pass looks for one class of failure.

## Pass 1: Identity

Confirm the correct client, property address, inspection date, report type, inspector, and company information.

If identity is wrong, stop and correct it before reviewing anything else.

## Pass 2: Completeness

Check for unresolved placeholders, temporary notes, unfinished sections, required limitations, and intended systems or sections that are accidentally absent.

A clean-looking report can still be incomplete if the workflow allowed an unanswered field or field note to disappear into the final export.

## Pass 3: High-priority findings

Read the findings that will matter most to the client.

Ask whether the location is accurate, the observed condition is specific, certainty matches the evidence, significance is proportionate, the recommendation follows from the finding, and urgency is justified rather than created by dramatic wording.

## Pass 4: Property-specific variables

Reusable narratives are efficient precisely because much of the wording is familiar. That also makes wrong variables easy to overlook.

Sweep:

**Where? What? How many? What happened? How certain? What action? Which photo?**

Pay special attention to room, component, quantity, material, extent, test result, recommendation, referral, and urgency.

Ask whether any sentence could have been copied from another property and survived without enough editing.

## Pass 5: Photographs

For report images, verify correct property, correct finding, correct location, useful context, useful detail, no unnecessary duplicates, and accurate annotations.

A close-up should not create a stronger claim than the narrative supports, and an annotation should not convert an unconfirmed source into a stated cause.

## Pass 6: Summary and consistency

A summary may compress a finding. It should not escalate it.

Check that summary inclusion is justified, the category is correct, qualifications and uncertainty are preserved, and any photographs still match.

Then search for contradictions and accidental duplicates.

Keep terminology stable enough that the same component or room does not appear to change identity across the report.

## Pass 7: Rendered output and customer experience

Review the final exported report rather than trusting only the editing screen.

Check for clipping or broken layout, image rendering, links where used, whether important findings are easy to locate, whether headings create useful hierarchy, whether boilerplate buries property-specific information, whether recommendations are actionable, and whether the final file is the correct file.

If practical, open the report on a phone-sized screen. Many clients will read at least part of it that way.

## Read it from two perspectives

After the targeted passes, ask two broader questions.

**As the client:** Can I tell what matters, find the complete explanation, understand uncertainty, and know what happens next?

**As another inspector:** Can I reconstruct the location, component, observation, level of certainty, and reason for the recommendation without having been at the property?

Those perspectives expose different weaknesses.

## Turn errors into system improvements

When QA catches an error, do not stop at correcting the sentence.

Classify why the system allowed it: capture failure, template failure, retrieval failure, classification failure, or review failure.

Then change the upstream process where appropriate.

The best QA system does more than repair today's report. It makes the same kind of error less likely to appear tomorrow.
