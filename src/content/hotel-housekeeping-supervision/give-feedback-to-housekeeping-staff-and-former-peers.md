Promotion changes the relationship with people who may have worked beside you for years.

Yesterday you swapped rooms, shared complaints, or solved problems as peers. Today you may assign their board, inspect their rooms, change a priority, or give corrective feedback.

Trying to pretend nothing changed usually does not help. Becoming cold or overly authoritative does not help either.

The useful goal is consistency.

## Keep feedback about observable work

The purpose of corrective feedback is to change work.

Start with something that can be checked:

- a visible defect
- a missing required setup
- a repeated inspection miss
- a board that is behind comparable work without a known block
- an update that was not completed
- a process step that is being skipped

Avoid personality labels.

"Careless," "lazy," or "difficult" do not tell the employee what needs to change.

A specific observation does.

## Use four parts

A practical feedback structure is:

**Observation → Standard → Action → Follow-up**

### Observation

Describe what happened.

Keep it concrete enough that both people know what event or work you are discussing.

### Standard

State the required standard or approved process.

Use the hotel's actual documentation, training, or expectation. Do not invent a personal rule and present it as property policy.

### Action

Say what needs to happen next.

For a simple room defect, that may be correcting the room before release.

For a repeated misunderstanding, it may be showing how the employee uses the final room check.

### Follow-up

Check whether the change held.

Without follow-up, coaching becomes a series of disconnected conversations.

## Separate one miss from a pattern

One defect is not automatically a performance problem.

A repeat issue may come from:

- an employee knowledge gap
- inconsistent training
- a missing or outdated checklist
- stock stored in the wrong place
- equipment problems
- a wider process failure

Record enough evidence to distinguish memory from a real pattern.

When several employees make the same mistake, investigate the system before assuming several people independently became careless at the same time.

## Former coworkers: explain the work, not your status

When a former peer challenges an allocation or change, keep the immediate conversation on the operational reason.

If the board is genuinely unfair, correct it.

If the allocation is reasonable, explain the work reason and keep the standard consistent.

The aim is not to win every disagreement. It is to apply the same operational test regardless of who raises the concern.

## Watch for overcorrection

New supervisors sometimes become harsher with friends to prove they are not showing favouritism.

That creates a different form of inconsistency.

Fairness means applying legitimate work reasons predictably. It does not mean treating every person identically regardless of training, authorised restrictions, specialist capability, or the actual assignment.

## Do not make your strongest employee the permanent rescue plan

A reliable employee may become the automatic answer whenever the shift breaks.

That can create resentment and hide a team capability gap.

Review who receives extra rooms, complex assignments, priority work, and disruption across several shifts. If one person is repeatedly carrying the hardest changes, check whether the pattern is genuinely justified.

## Know when routine coaching ends

Some issues move beyond ordinary supervisor feedback.

Formal discipline, serious conduct, attendance processes, safety matters, protected information, or other significant concerns should follow the employer's actual management, HR, security, or safety process.

Do not invent your own disciplinary route.

A good supervisor knows both how to give ordinary feedback and when the issue now belongs somewhere else.
