Labor estimating is difficult because the estimator must form an opinion about how long the work will take.

The arithmetic is simple. The assumption behind the hours is the hard part.

For a first-job estimate, a useful structure is:

**activity → crew → time → person-hours → labor cost**

That makes the assumption visible enough to challenge before the bid and compare after the job.

## Define the activity before entering hours

"Framing labor — 96 hours" is a number.

It is more useful when the estimate explains what those hours are expected to accomplish.

Break a meaningful work package into operating steps such as layout and staging, primary installation, handling or setup, fitting around openings or interfaces, and cleanup or small completion items.

The exact breakdown depends on the work. The principle is to describe the activity clearly enough that another person can understand what the crew is expected to do.

## Use the best evidence you actually have

When a contractor has little clean history, do not invent precision.

A practical evidence ladder is:

1. your own comparable history, if you have it;
2. crew or supervisor input;
3. relevant supplier or manufacturer information;
4. comparable completed work;
5. a clearly documented provisional assumption.

A weak source is not automatically useless. The mistake is turning a weak source into a precise-looking number with no note explaining where it came from.

Record the basis beside important hour assumptions.

## Convert crew and duration into person-hours

Suppose you believe a task needs two people for six hours.

The estimate should carry:

**2 people × 6 hours = 12 person-hours**

This separates human effort from calendar duration.

Person-hours do not, by themselves, establish the project schedule. That distinction matters when you sanity-check a total.

## Translate the total back into operations

After building the activity hours, convert the total into person-days or an approximate crew plan as a reasonableness check.

If a work package totals 96 person-hours, that equals twelve eight-hour person-days.

That does not prove a four-person crew will complete the package in exactly three calendar days. It gives the estimator another way to ask whether the human effort fits the amount of work represented.

If the answer feels implausible, return to the activity assumptions rather than adding an unexplained percentage.

## Wage is not automatically labor cost

An hourly wage is not necessarily the complete internal labor cost to the business.

A real contractor should derive labor cost from its own payroll, employment costs, and accounting method.

The same cost should not then be recovered a second time somewhere else simply because it also appears on an overhead checklist.

Keep wage, employer labor burden or other employment cost, internal labor cost, selling price or charge-out method, and company overhead conceptually separate.

Where accounting treatment is uncertain, confirm it with the appropriate bookkeeper or accountant rather than copying a percentage from another company.

## Name the conditions that can change productivity

Labor performance can change because of:

- access;
- staging;
- working height;
- crew experience;
- congestion;
- sequencing;
- weather;
- travel;
- rework;
- equipment availability;
- occupied conditions;
- changes in scope.

If one of these conditions matters to an important hour assumption, name it.

A visible condition can later be checked. A generic contingency cannot explain what actually happened.

## Do not hide uncertainty with "labor equals materials"

A shortcut such as "labor is the same as material cost" disconnects the labor estimate from the work the crew must perform.

Material prices can move without the work becoming faster or slower. Likewise, a labor-intensive detail can change without a proportional change in material cost.

A work-based labor build-up is easier to defend, update, and improve.

## Compare actual hours by activity

After the job, do not look only at the total labor variance.

Compare activities or work packages where the records allow it.

If siding labor ran above the estimate, ask why. Was the measured quantity wrong? Did the scope change? Were there more openings or trim complexity? Was staging poor? Did material handling take longer? Was unrelated work charged to the same code?

The useful lesson is the cause, not just the percentage over or under.

## A labor build-up checklist

For each important labor package:

- define the activity;
- state the measurable scope or work steps;
- record the evidence behind the duration;
- state crew size and duration;
- convert to person-hours;
- apply the company's actual internal labor-cost method;
- translate the total back into person-days or a plausible crew plan;
- list project conditions most likely to alter productivity;
- compare actual hours later;
- record the cause before changing the next estimate.

That creates an operating story behind the labor number.

When the estimate reaches company-level treatment, keep the labor cost base distinct from [overhead, markup, and margin](/construction-estimating/construction-markup-vs-margin/).
