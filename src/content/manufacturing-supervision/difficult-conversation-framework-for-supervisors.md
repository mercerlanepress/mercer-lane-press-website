Many routine supervisory problems become difficult because they are left alone until frustration has accumulated.

One incomplete handover becomes several. A late start becomes a pattern. A sharp comment becomes normal. Then the supervisor feels as though the conversation has to solve weeks of irritation in one sitting.

A better approach is to deal with ordinary issues early enough that you can still describe what happened clearly.

Use this sequence:

**Prepare → Describe → Clarify → Listen → Respond → Confirm → Follow Up**

It is a structure, not a script.

## 1. Prepare before you speak

Before starting, ask:

- What happened?
- What evidence do I actually have?
- What am I assuming?
- What legitimate standard applies?
- How would the employee reasonably know that standard?
- What outcome do I need?
- Is this within my authority?
- Does a formal or specialist process already apply?
- What question should I ask first?

If your notes contain labels such as **lazy**, **careless**, **difficult**, or **disrespectful**, rewrite them as observations.

“Three handovers were missing the outstanding-work section” gives both people something specific to discuss.

“Your attitude to handovers is terrible” starts with a judgement.

## 2. Describe the event or pattern

Open with the issue, not with a verdict about the person.

Useful openings are specific enough that the employee knows what you mean:

- “I want to talk about the last three start times.”
- “Can we go through what happened on Line 2 after lunch?”
- “I want to discuss the way the allocation disagreement happened in front of the team.”

Avoid dramatic language unless the situation and your organisation’s process genuinely require it.

The first thirty seconds should identify the subject, not prosecute the employee.

## 3. Clarify the legitimate expectation

A correction is stronger when the standard underneath it is clear.

Ask yourself whether the expectation is:

- a formal requirement;
- an agreed local expectation;
- your preferred method;
- simply a long-standing habit.

Do not present personal preference as policy.

A useful clarification explains what needs to happen and why it matters operationally.

For example, a handover field may matter because the incoming shift needs to know what remains unfinished. That is much clearer than saying the employee needs to “take handovers more seriously.”

## 4. Listen for information that changes the problem

After stating the issue, ask what happened.

Then listen.

You may discover:

- unclear instruction;
- inadequate time;
- equipment trouble;
- material delay;
- training or competence gaps;
- conflicting priorities;
- a misunderstanding;
- deliberate disregard;
- a health or personal matter that moves the issue into another process;
- evidence showing your original conclusion was wrong.

Do not ask a question if you have already decided that no answer can change your view.

The purpose of listening is not to surrender the standard. It is to understand what problem you are actually managing.

## 5. Respond to the problem you now have

Once you know more, choose the next step that belongs within your role.

That might be:

- clarify an expectation;
- remove a work obstacle;
- provide normal coaching;
- agree a practical action;
- correct inconsistent supervision;
- verify a record;
- ask another department for help;
- pause and use a formal process;
- seek advice before continuing.

If the employee raises information involving health, disability, harassment, discrimination, safeguarding, serious misconduct, or another issue beyond ordinary supervision, do not improvise your own process. Use the appropriate organisational route.

The [supervisor authority and escalation guide](/manufacturing-supervision/supervisor-authority-map-and-escalation/) can help you decide when the category has changed.

## 6. Confirm what happens next

Do not finish with a vague “Okay, just make sure it improves.”

Confirm:

- what is expected;
- what the employee will do;
- what you will do;
- what support or escalation is required;
- when you will check back.

The employee does not have to agree emotionally for the next step to be clear.

A realistic ending may be that the employee still disagrees with the target, allocation, or standard. Your job is to make the current expectation and the next review point understandable.

## 7. Follow up

Follow-up is where a conversation becomes management rather than talk.

Ask afterwards:

- Did I learn anything that changes the issue?
- Did I make the expectation clear?
- Did I promise anything?
- Does another process now apply?
- When will I check again?

If you never return to the subject, the employee may reasonably decide that it was not important.

## What if the employee says, “Everyone does it”?

Do not let a wider consistency issue erase the specific conversation.

A useful response is to acknowledge both:

- the individual examples still need to be understood;
- if the standard is being applied inconsistently, that broader problem also needs attention.

Deal with the evidence in front of you, then check whether the system is inconsistent.

## What if the employee says, “You’re picking on me”?

Do not defend your character.

Return to specifics:

- why this conversation is happening;
- which examples you are discussing;
- what standard applies;
- what concern the employee wants you to hear.

If the employee has a separate complaint about your behaviour, hear it rather than automatically dismissing it. If it is unrelated, schedule it separately so one conversation does not become five.

## What if the employee says you are wrong?

Verify.

If the record, system, or evidence shows that your conclusion was wrong, correct it.

Do not manufacture a new criticism to save face.

A supervisor can say, “You’re right. I drew the wrong conclusion.” That does not remove responsibility for any separate issue that still exists.

## What if the employee becomes emotional?

Do not rush simply because silence feels uncomfortable.

Give the person a moment where appropriate. If new information changes the category of the issue, pause and use the correct support or formal route.

You are not required to diagnose or personally resolve every matter that emerges from a routine conversation.

## Avoid the twelve-complaint conversation

When feedback has been delayed, supervisors sometimes arrive with a long list of old frustrations.

That usually weakens the discussion.

Where possible, address routine issues close enough to the event that both people can remember what happened. If several events form a genuine pattern, use representative evidence and explain the pattern rather than unloading every remembered complaint.

## Use facts before conclusions

A practical self-check is:

1. What did I directly observe?
2. What legitimate records support it?
3. What am I assuming?
4. What do I still need to ask?
5. Is this mainly a People, Work, Clarity, or Authority problem?

For performance figures, add a fifth question: **Is the measurement itself fair and meaningful?**

That sequence keeps the conversation anchored to something that can be checked.
