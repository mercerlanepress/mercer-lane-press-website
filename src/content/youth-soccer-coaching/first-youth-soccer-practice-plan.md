The first practice has a more useful job than showing that you know a large number of drills.

It should make the **second practice easier**.

You need to learn names, see how comfortable players are with the ball, notice who seeks or avoids involvement, find out whether your space is too large or too small, and discover how well the group responds to short instructions.

The players are also learning what practice with you feels like.

For a recreational youth team, a simple first session is usually more useful than an elaborate one.

## Before players arrive

Confirm the basics before you build the session:

- the correct practice time and field;
- whatever coach clearances or training your organization requires;
- the current emergency, injury, concussion and safeguarding procedures;
- the equipment the club supplies;
- the communication method parents should use.

Arrive with enough time to make a small playing area and prepare the first activity before parents start asking questions.

For a basic session, balls, cones or field markers, pinnies if available, a pump, and any required safety or first-aid equipment may be enough.

Do not buy equipment simply because you are uncertain about coaching.

## A simple 60-minute first practice

Adjust the times to the age group, field, attendance and guidance from your organization.

### 1. Arrival play — about 10 minutes

Do not make the first few players sit and wait for everybody else.

If you have enough players, start 1v1, 2v2 or 3v3 to small goals and add children as they arrive.

If a game is not possible yet, give each child a ball and a safe area in which to dribble and explore.

Your main job is to greet families, learn names and watch.

### 2. Very short introduction — about 2 minutes

Bring the group together only long enough to establish the operating expectations.

Keep the message simple:

- be safe;
- treat teammates properly;
- stop briefly when the coach needs to give an instruction;
- get back to playing quickly.

Then start again.

A first practice does not need a speech about the season.

### 3. Ball and space — about 10 minutes

Give each player a ball where equipment allows.

Ask for actions that let you observe rather than lecture:

- move without colliding;
- keep the ball close enough to change direction;
- find open space;
- turn at a line;
- look up between touches.

Choose one or two cues. Do not try to teach everything about dribbling in ten minutes.

### 4. Small-sided game — about 15 minutes

Use small teams.

Watch what happens when the game becomes less controlled.

Do players spread out or all chase the ball?

Who wants the ball?

Who hides from it?

Does one player dominate every possession?

Are some children confused by the direction of play?

This evidence is more useful than a perfect diagram.

## Pick one problem, not six

Suppose everyone crowds around the ball.

You could stop and explain the entire idea of team shape.

A better first intervention is smaller:

**When your teammate has the ball, can you find some space where they could pass to you?**

Widen the playing area if necessary and let the game give players another chance to solve the problem.

If the main issue is different, change one thing that helps with that problem.

## 5. One simple practice challenge — about 10 minutes

Connect the challenge to what you just observed.

If players are reluctant to dribble, create a game where moving with the ball matters.

If they struggle to change direction, use a simple ball-control task.

If the activity is failing because it is too crowded, enlarge the area.

The principle is more important than the exact drill:

**change the environment so players get repeated chances to work on the problem.**

## 6. Play again — 10 to 15 minutes

Return to a normal small-sided game.

Use one short reminder from the earlier activity.

Then coach less.

The final game is not filler. It lets you see whether anything from the session appears when the players have real choices again.

## 7. Finish — about 2 minutes

Ask one short question such as:

**What helped your team today?**

Take one or two answers.

Remind players of the next session, collect the equipment, and finish.

## Write three notes before you forget

After practice, record:

**KEEP** — something that worked.

**CHANGE** — something that made the session harder.

**TRY** — one thing to work on next time.

Those three lines give you the start of your next plan.

For a repeatable method, use the [10-minute youth soccer practice builder](/youth-soccer-coaching/youth-soccer-practice-plan-in-ten-minutes/).

The first practice does not need to be impressive.

If children were safe, active, involved in soccer, treated fairly, and gave you useful information for next time, the session has already done important work.
