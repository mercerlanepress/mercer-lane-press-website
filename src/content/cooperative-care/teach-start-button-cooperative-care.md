A start-button behavior gives a dog a simple action that begins a handling repetition.

Common choices include:

- chin resting in your hand;
- chin resting on a folded towel;
- standing on a mat;
- presenting a paw for a brief inspection.

The behavior is useful because it makes the session more predictable.

It is not a contract that allows unlimited handling until the dog finally breaks position.

## Choose a position that fits the task

A chin rest can work well for face, ear, and examination preparation.

A mat can suit brushing, drying, general body handling, and standing examinations.

A paw presentation can support paw inspection once the underlying handling is already comfortable.

Choose a position that is physically easy for the dog and practical for the procedure.

If a dog has pain, orthopedic limitations, neck problems, or another health concern, the comfortable position may need veterinary guidance.

## Teach the behavior before adding care

Do not bring out the difficult equipment yet.

For a hand chin rest:

1. Sit beside the dog in a comfortable, non-slip area.
2. Hold an open palm a short distance below the chin.
3. Mark and reward investigation or a small lowering of the head.
4. Gradually reinforce closer approximations.
5. Build one second of relaxed contact.
6. Add a clear release such as "all done."
7. Move your hand away and let the dog leave the position.

Do not push your hand upward into the jaw to manufacture the behavior.

The useful part is that the dog places the head voluntarily.

## A towel chin rest can free both hands

A folded towel on your thigh or another stable surface can become the target.

Reinforce the dog for placing the chin on the towel.

Check the height. The neck should look comfortable rather than stretched downward or forced upward.

Build duration gradually before any difficult touch is added.

## A mat can be the start location

For a mat station:

1. Put down a secure, non-slip mat.
2. Mark and reward looking at or approaching it.
3. Reinforce stepping onto it.
4. Build a brief relaxed stand, sit, or lie if that position suits the dog.
5. Give the release cue and encourage the dog off the mat.
6. Reward some releases so leaving is part of the routine.

During training, do not block the exit simply because the dog knows the mat behavior.

## Add one easy touch

Once the start behavior is easy by itself, add a neutral handling action on a body area the dog already accepts.

A first session might look like:

- chin down;
- touch shoulder for half a second;
- mark;
- reward;
- release.

Then perhaps:

- chin down;
- touch shoulder for one second;
- mark;
- reward;
- release.

Only one variable should change at a time.

If the dog lifts the chin as your hand moves down the leg, the dog has used the system. Remove your hand and make the next repetition easier.

## Keep the release separate from opting out

The release cue tells the dog that **you** are ending the repetition.

An opt-out is the dog choosing to leave before you release.

Both should stop the training action.

Why have both?

A predictable release reduces the need for escape. The opt-out keeps communication available when the current step becomes too difficult.

## Do not turn the start button into restraint

A common mistake is to teach a beautiful chin rest and then quietly change the rules.

The dog keeps the chin down for one brush stroke, so the owner does three. Then six. Then keeps going because the dog has not technically lifted the chin.

That turns a voluntary behavior into an endurance task.

Instead, release before the dog needs to leave.

Short successful repetitions can build a stronger history than one long session that ends only when the dog has had enough.

## Read the whole dog, not only the start behavior

A dog can keep a chin down while the rest of the body becomes tense.

The start button is one channel of information.

Also watch:

- facial tension;
- breathing;
- weight shift;
- paw or body withdrawal;
- food-taking quality;
- freezing;
- willingness to return after release.

If the start behavior and the rest of the dog disagree, follow the more cautious signal.

The guide to [reading dog stress during handling](/cooperative-care/read-dog-stress-during-handling/) gives a simple green, amber, and red framework.

## Know when the behavior is ready for harder work

Before increasing difficulty, look for:

- voluntary start without luring;
- loose rather than frozen posture;
- normal reinforcement-taking if food is used;
- voluntary return after release;
- several easy repetitions at the current touch or equipment level.

Do not progress simply because the dog physically remained in place.

## Start buttons in real veterinary care

A clinic may need a different position or may have medical and safety constraints that do not exist at home.

Tell the veterinary team what the dog knows, but do not expect a trained start behavior to override clinical judgment.

Useful information is specific:

- the dog uses a chin rest to begin handling;
- lifting the chin means pause during training;
- the dog works best on a non-slip surface;
- the dog has particular early stress signals;
- the dog may need a different clinical plan if handling becomes unsafe.
