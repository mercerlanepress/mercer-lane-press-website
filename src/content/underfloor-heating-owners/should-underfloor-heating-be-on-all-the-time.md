**No: underfloor heating does not always have to run 24 hours a day. But switching it off whenever you leave is not automatically best either.** The right schedule depends on the heat source, floor construction, building heat loss, controller logic, occupancy and required comfort.

## Continuous comfort is not continuous heating output

A thermostat can remain set to a comfortable target for most of the day while calling for heat only intermittently. Likewise, a long permitted operating window does not necessarily mean a boiler or heat pump is delivering full output constantly.

A wet underfloor system embedded in heavy screed can respond slowly because the floor stores energy. A lightweight overlay may respond faster. An electric bathroom mat can have very different costs and controls from a water system supplied by a heat pump.

A universal 'always on saves money' rule ignores those differences.

## Four questions before choosing a programme

1. **When must the room be comfortable?** Write the times people actually use it rather than starting with the heating-on time.
2. **How long does this room respond?** Use the manufacturer's guidance and your own observations on reasonably comparable days. Do not assume every floor warms in the same number of minutes.
3. **What produces the heat?** Boiler, heat pump and electric resistance systems respond to operating conditions differently. Some heat-pump control strategies are commissioned for stable low-temperature operation.
4. **What does the controller's time mean?** It may start a programmed target at the stated time or aim to meet comfort by that time through adaptive start.

Do not alter an installer-protected heating curve, mixing valve, flow limit or manifold balancing to accelerate a room.

## What is a setback?

A **setback** is a lower target temperature during sleep or absence, rather than a complete shutdown. A lower indoor temperature can reduce building heat loss while it remains lower, but recovering comfort takes time and energy. Heat-source efficiency and other controls affect the comparison.

A big nighttime setback in a heavy floor may lead to a cold breakfast. No setback may be appropriate for some commissioned strategies. Neither approach is guaranteed cheaper for a particular house.

Never choose a setting that creates unsafe cold, damp, condensation, freezing risks or unsuitable conditions for occupants.

## Compare a schedule change sensibly

If your exact user manual permits schedule changes and comfort/safety conditions allow it:

- Save the existing programme so you can restore it.
- Log actual room temperatures and when comfort is reached across several relevant days.
- Change **one small, reversible ordinary user setting**.
- Observe the outcome under reasonably comparable weather and routines.
- Track actual energy readings where possible, but also note hot-water use, occupancy and tariffs.
- Conclude 'comfort improved' only where observations support that; do not claim a percentage energy saving without adequate evidence.

Comparing one mild week with a colder week does not isolate the effect of a timetable change. Equally, a bill containing domestic hot water and cooking energy is not a direct underfloor-only meter.
