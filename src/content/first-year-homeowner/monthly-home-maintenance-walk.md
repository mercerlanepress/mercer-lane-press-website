A monthly maintenance walk does not need to consume a weekend.

Its value comes from **repetition**.

A professional may know much more about a building system. You have a different advantage: you live in the property and can notice that a stain is new, a sound changed, a door now behaves differently, or water appears only after a particular kind of rain.

The walk turns that familiarity into usable evidence.

## Use the same route whenever practical

Choose a sequence you can repeat.

For example:

**Inside → utility areas → life-safety points → outside perimeter**

The exact route matters less than consistency. When you look at the same places in roughly the same order, change becomes easier to see.

Take:

- your phone or camera;
- a flashlight;
- your Home Record or notes;
- any previous photograph you need for comparison.

This is not a professional home inspection. Do not dismantle equipment, enter unsafe spaces, climb onto the roof, or turn an observation checklist into permission to perform work beyond your competence.

## Inside: look up, look down, and check water-connected areas

### Ceilings and walls

Look for new or changed:

- staining;
- peeling;
- cracking;
- sagging;
- discoloration.

Do not diagnose from appearance alone.

A ceiling stain might relate to plumbing, rainwater, condensation, mechanical equipment, or an old repaired condition. Your first questions are simpler:

- Is it wet now?
- Is it changing?
- What is above or near it?
- Does it correspond with rain or water use?
- Was it visible in earlier photographs?

If moisture is part of the observation, continue with the [water and moisture warning-sign guide](/first-year-homeowner/water-and-moisture-warning-signs-homeowners/).

### Accessible sinks and toilets

Open sink cabinets where access is ordinary and safe.

Look for dampness, droplets, corrosion, staining, swelling, or a change from the previous month.

Around toilets, notice the accessible supply connection, the floor around the base, and persistent running.

Do not repeatedly tighten unfamiliar plumbing parts or dismantle fittings simply because a checklist told you to inspect them.

### Water-connected appliances

Look around the washing machine, dishwasher, refrigerator water connection where present, and other accessible water-connected equipment.

You are interested in the **connection to the house** as well as the appliance itself.

A small active leak from a pressurized connection deserves a different response from an old dry stain.

### Water-heater and HVAC areas

Is the surrounding area still dry?

Has corrosion, staining, unusual noise, vibration, odor, or water appeared?

For heating and cooling equipment, compare normal operation with what you have heard and seen before. Do not open service compartments to investigate a new symptom.

### Known cracks and monitored conditions

Any item you intentionally monitor should have a baseline and review rule.

Compare it with the earlier photograph.

Record meaningful change rather than relying on memory.

If something has remained stable and no longer needs active monitoring, close the item instead of carrying an endless list.

## Life safety: check access and follow the designed procedures

Confirm that:

- alarm locations remain accessible;
- exits remain usable;
- the electrical panel is not blocked by storage;
- the main water control remains accessible;
- important utility and emergency information can still be found.

Test alarms and other designed safety devices only according to current applicable guidance and manufacturer instructions.

If a carbon-monoxide alarm activates, follow the alarm and emergency guidance rather than remaining inside to diagnose the source.

## Outside: read the building as a water-management system

Start from the ground.

Look at the roof from safe viewpoints rather than climbing onto it.

Then check:

- gutters and downspouts;
- exterior walls;
- windows and doors;
- the foundation perimeter;
- visible drainage;
- steps and railings;
- vegetation near the building;
- outdoor HVAC equipment;
- obvious pest-entry points.

For rainwater, use the route:

**Roof → gutter → downspout → ground → away**

A downspout is not the end of the question. Notice where the water goes next.

Look for ponding, erosion, disconnection, overflow staining, vegetation blocking inspection, branches contacting the building, or new deterioration.

## After ordinary rain, make one extra observation when safe

Dry-weather inspection cannot show you how every drainage route behaves.

During ordinary rain—not lightning, dangerous wind, flooding, or severe weather—observe from safe locations:

- gutter behavior;
- downspout discharge;
- ponding beside the house;
- runoff crossing doors or paved areas;
- previously damp basement or crawlspace locations;
- known leak points.

Do not go outside in dangerous conditions just to complete a maintenance exercise.

The purpose is to learn how the property behaves under real weather, not to finish a checklist at any cost.

## Finish with a decision, not just a note

Every observation should end in one of a small number of states:

**No action** — ordinary condition, no useful follow-up.

**Monitor** — defined condition, baseline, review date, escalation trigger.

**Schedule** — known work that can reasonably be planned.

**Prompt attention** — an important condition that should not be left indefinitely.

**Emergency** — immediate risk to people or major property; routine inspection stops and the appropriate emergency response takes priority.

For non-emergency uncertainty, use [DIY or call a professional?](/first-year-homeowner/diy-or-call-a-professional-home-repair/) before turning an observation into a repair project.

## Keep the walk short by making it property-specific

Your first few walks may take longer because you are still learning the house.

Over time, remove tasks that do not apply and add only requirements that come from:

- the equipment actually installed;
- manufacturer instructions;
- local climate;
- qualified professional recommendations;
- conditions you have a reason to monitor.

A mature maintenance system should become more specific and often quieter.

The point of the monthly walk is not to discover a new project every thirty days.

It is to make meaningful change harder to miss.
