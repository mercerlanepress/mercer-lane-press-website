Being promoted over people who were recently your coworkers creates a management problem that an external hire does not have.

Your team remembers what you complained about, which jobs you disliked, who you socialised with, and what you used to say when you had no supervisory responsibility.

Pretending that nothing has changed is rarely sustainable.

The useful goal is not to become distant. It is to keep relationships human while making work decisions on defensible work grounds.

## Friendship does not have to end

A promotion does not require a personality transplant.

You can still speak normally, laugh with the team, admit mistakes, ask for help, and recognise expertise.

What changes is your responsibility when work decisions conflict with friendship.

Imagine a former coworker asks for an easier assignment because you are friends.

Before answering, ask:

- Would I make the same decision for another employee in comparable circumstances?
- What production, competence, workload, rotation, or training reason supports the allocation?
- Is there a proper route for the flexibility being requested?
- Am I using private friendship information to make a workplace decision?

If the request is legitimate, handle it through the same route available to others. If it is not, friendship is not a work reason for granting it.

## Do not overcorrect either

Some new supervisors become harsher with friends to prove that they are independent.

That is not consistency.

Giving a former friend worse assignments purely to avoid the appearance of favouritism is another way of letting the friendship distort the decision.

The standard is simpler:

**Use legitimate work reasons and apply them consistently.**

## Explain the changed responsibility

A former peer may say:

**“You would have done the same thing six months ago.”**

You do not need to rewrite history.

You can acknowledge the past and explain the present:

- you did not hold responsibility for the shift before;
- you now have to keep one clear plan;
- you are accountable for handover, allocation, standards, or follow-up;
- disagreement can still be raised, but work decisions need an agreed route.

That is more credible than pretending your earlier views never existed.

## Confidentiality changes after promotion

Before promotion, coworkers may have shared almost everything they knew.

As a supervisor, you may receive staffing information, employee concerns, management discussions, or other information you are not free to pass on.

Do not hint at confidential information to preserve social closeness.

A useful boundary is:

**If information can be shared, share it through the right route. If it cannot, do not trade hints for popularity.**

This can feel awkward at first. It is part of the role change.

## When a former peer challenges you in front of the team

A public challenge can trigger the urge to prove authority because other people are watching.

Resist that.

First ask whether the person is raising a real operational issue.

An experienced employee who says your plan creates a technical problem may be giving you valuable information, even if the delivery is poor.

Separate two questions:

1. Is the operational point correct?
2. Was the way it was raised acceptable?

If the issue is disrupting the shift, move the disagreement out of the audience where practical. Then deal with the technical point and the conduct point separately.

You do not lose authority by correcting a decision when the evidence changes.

## Someone else wanted your job

A coworker who applied for the same promotion may be disappointed or may genuinely believe they were the stronger candidate.

Do not demand enthusiasm.

Do not spend weeks explaining why management chose you.

Focus on professional cooperation:

- recognise legitimate experience;
- invite specific information when you may have missed something;
- keep responsibility for the shift clear;
- address only the behaviour that actually occurs.

Do not treat resentment as a fact until it shows up in observable behaviour.

## Watch for hidden favouritism in ordinary decisions

Favouritism is not limited to obvious benefits.

It can appear through:

- giving friends advance information;
- moving them away from undesirable work more often;
- correcting their mistakes privately while correcting others publicly;
- overlooking attendance or conduct issues;
- giving them first choice of development opportunities;
- allowing them to challenge instructions in ways you would address with someone else.

Review actual decisions rather than relying on your intention to be fair.

Ask:

**Could I explain this decision to the whole team using legitimate work reasons?**

If the answer is no, the decision deserves another look.

## Fairness is not identical treatment

Equal treatment and fair treatment are not always the same.

If only one available employee is trained for a particular process, that can be a legitimate reason to assign them there more often.

If a rotation exists, follow it consistently. If competence or production need changes the rotation, be able to explain why.

The supervisor’s job is not to make every allocation look identical. It is to make the reasons legitimate and consistent.

## Use a former-peer boundary planner

For any relationship likely to become difficult, write down:

- the relationship before promotion;
- the likely pressure point;
- what changed because of the role;
- what did not change;
- a boundary that may need explaining;
- the favouritism risk;
- the overcorrection risk;
- the wording you will use if the issue appears.

This is especially useful before the first awkward request, not after several exceptions have accumulated.

## If the friendship changes anyway

Sometimes promotion changes a relationship even when you handle it carefully.

A former coworker may become more guarded or stop socialising with you.

You cannot force the old relationship back by weakening supervisory boundaries.

You can remain respectful, approachable, consistent, and human. Professional cooperation is enough.
