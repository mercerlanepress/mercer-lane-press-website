One of the easiest traps for a newly promoted manufacturing supervisor is to keep doing the work that earned the promotion.

You know how to solve the technical problem. You can complete the changeover faster. You can clear the fault more efficiently. When the shift comes under pressure, taking over feels useful.

The immediate task may get finished.

The longer-term cost is that every difficult task keeps returning to you.

Delegation is how a team becomes less dependent on constant supervisory rescue. It works only when responsibility, authority, boundaries, and follow-up are clear.

## Use six delegation questions

Before handing over a task, clarify:

### 1. What is the task?

Be specific about what needs doing.

“Sort Line 2” is not a useful delegation instruction.

### 2. What outcome matters?

Define the result rather than every movement.

A competent employee may reach the required outcome in a different way from you while still following the correct standard and procedure.

### 3. What authority does the employee have?

Say which decisions they can make without returning to you.

Delegation fails when somebody is given responsibility but no practical authority to act.

### 4. What constraints apply?

Clarify relevant:

- safety rules;
- quality requirements;
- process instructions;
- competence limits;
- staffing boundaries;
- approval requirements;
- escalation points.

Do not delegate permission to bypass formal controls.

### 5. When will you check in?

Agree a checkpoint instead of hovering.

The check-in should reflect the risk, complexity, employee experience, and whether the work is new or routine.

### 6. How will completion be confirmed?

Decide how you will know that the task is actually finished.

That might be a normal record, a handover update, an agreed result, or another established method used by the site.

## Delegation is not dumping

Dumping sounds like:

**“Can you just sort this?”**

The employee is left to discover the real objective, authority, constraints, and success criteria.

Delegation gives enough clarity for someone to own a legitimate piece of work.

It also avoids passing formal management duties to another employee simply because you dislike doing them.

A supervisor remains accountable for deciding what can reasonably be delegated.

## Do not rescue too quickly

When someone is struggling, a new supervisor’s first instinct may be to step in.

Before taking over, ask:

- What have you tried?
- What do you think the next step is?
- What information are you missing?
- What do you need from me to continue?

If the employee is competent and the task remains within normal safe limits, one useful question may develop more capability than taking the task back.

That changes when safety, quality, technical procedure, or competence requires intervention. Development is never a reason to leave someone operating outside the boundaries that apply.

## Know when helping is genuinely the right decision

Supervisors sometimes need to work directly.

A small operation may expect the supervisor to perform substantial operational work. A major disruption may require everyone available to support recovery within their competence and local procedures.

If you step in, make the trade-off visible.

Ask:

- Who is now watching the wider shift?
- Which supervisory decisions may be delayed?
- Could somebody else own this task?
- What must I return to immediately afterwards?
- Is this genuinely temporary?
- Why did the shift require me specifically?

Helping is not the problem.

Using technical work to escape supervisory work is.

## What reasonable oversight looks like

Oversight is usually connected to a reason such as:

- risk;
- a new task;
- an inexperienced employee;
- a known recent problem;
- agreed checkpoints;
- performance drift;
- a required control.

Micromanagement is more likely when the supervisor:

- checks constantly without a specific reason;
- dictates harmless personal method choices;
- takes back decisions already delegated;
- demands continuous updates from competent staff;
- corrects people simply because they work differently.

The distinction is not whether an employee dislikes being checked. It is whether the oversight is proportionate and connected to the work.

## If someone says you are micromanaging

Do not argue about the label.

Ask for an example.

If the employee says you return every twenty minutes, consider whether the checking is genuinely necessary.

You might replace repeated interruption with an agreed checkpoint at the end of the run.

If the employee says you are micromanaging because you asked why required checks were missing, return to the actual issue. You are not asking them to copy your preferred method; you are following up a legitimate standard.

A label should not replace the work question.

## Increase oversight with an exit point

After a mistake or repeated missed requirement, closer checking may be justified.

But define what allows normal oversight to resume.

For example:

- review the next three records;
- check the next two changeovers;
- use a midpoint check until competence is confirmed;
- return to normal once the agreed standard is met consistently.

Without an exit point, temporary oversight can quietly become permanent control.

## Delegate to develop capability

Delegation is not only workload relief.

It gives capable employees the chance to coordinate, decide, and learn within legitimate boundaries.

After delegated work, ask:

- What went as planned?
- Where did you need to make a decision?
- What information was missing?
- What would you do differently next time?

Then add specific feedback.

If the result was strong, name the behaviour worth repeating. If something needs development, identify the part that should change next time.

Do not turn every delegated task into a formal assessment.

## Use experienced employees without surrendering responsibility

Experienced operators may know more than the supervisor about a particular process.

Ask for their reasoning.

Use their expertise.

But do not make them the hidden decision-maker for the shift.

A useful distinction is:

**Expertise can be delegated and consulted. Formal responsibility still needs to remain clear.**

## Review your own dependence pattern

At the end of the week, ask:

- What did I personally complete that someone else could have handled?
- Where does the team still wait for my answer unnecessarily?
- Which tasks repeatedly pull me away from supervisory work?
- What knowledge sits with only one person?
- What could be delegated one level more next week?

If the shift struggles whenever you are absent, that can be evidence of weak delegation, weak documentation, training gaps, or process dependence.

The goal is not to make the supervisor unnecessary.

It is to stop making the supervisor the only route through every ordinary decision.
