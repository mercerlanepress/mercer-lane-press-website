A settle mat is useful because it gives the dog a clear physical location that can travel from room to room.

The mat itself is not calming. Its value comes from the reinforcement history you build on it.

## Choose a simple mat

Use something that is:

- comfortable enough for the dog to lie on;
- non-slip on the intended floor;
- large enough for the dog's body;
- portable if you want to use it away from home;
- visually distinct from the surrounding floor.

A bath mat, folded blanket, small dog bed, or purpose-made mat can work.

If the dog chews or guards fabric, choose a safer setup and seek individualized advice where needed.

## Stage 1: make the mat valuable

Put the mat in a quiet room. Do not begin by repeatedly cueing “settle.”

When the dog looks at or moves toward the mat, mark the behavior and place a reward on the mat.

Raise the criterion gradually:

1. look at the mat;
2. one paw on;
3. two or more paws;
4. all four paws;
5. sit or stand on the mat;
6. lie down;
7. remain for a few seconds.

Let the dog discover the location rather than continuously luring with food under the nose.

After several successful repetitions, toss a reset treat a short distance away so the dog can leave and choose the mat again.

## Stage 2: add a cue after the behavior is predictable

When the dog is repeatedly moving to the mat before you say anything, add a cue such as “mat” or “settle.”

Say it once, then allow the dog to move toward the mat. Mark and reward arrival.

If you need to repeat the cue several times before movement begins, make the behavior easier again rather than making the cue louder.

## Stage 3: move from stationing to relaxing

A dog can learn to sprint onto the mat and stare at the treat pouch. That is useful stationing, but it is not yet settling.

Start to reinforce lower-energy behavior such as:

- lying on one hip;
- lowering the head;
- looking away;
- resting the chin;
- softer posture;
- longer pauses between checking in with you.

Deliver food low and quietly so the reward does not repeatedly pull the dog back into a standing position.

If food itself creates a lot of activation, space rewards farther apart and use the comfortable environment as part of the reinforcement.

## Stage 4: build duration without climbing every repetition

Do not increase time in a straight line.

A sequence might move from five seconds to eight, then twelve, back to ten, then fifteen. Mixing easier and harder repetitions prevents every attempt from becoming a new endurance test.

Reward before the dog is likely to leave and use a clear release such as “all done.”

The aim is comfortable duration, not a rigid stay.

## Stage 5: add tiny handler movement

Change one variable at a time:

- stand up;
- take one step;
- walk around a chair;
- move to a counter;
- sit at a table;
- briefly face away;
- carry an object;
- open a cupboard.

If the dog leaves the mat, calmly reduce the difficulty. Do not drag or physically place the dog back.

## Stage 6: add mild distractions

Only after the earlier layers are comfortable should you add manageable distractions such as:

- another person walking across the room;
- quiet television;
- a door opening elsewhere;
- food preparation at a distance;
- a familiar person entering calmly.

If activation rises, reduce the distraction.

The [five-zone escalation guide](/over-aroused-dog/signs-dog-over-aroused-escalation-zones/) can help you notice the point where practice should become easier.

## Stage 7: move the mat

A behavior learned in the living room does not automatically transfer to a porch, park, friend's home, or café.

Build a location ladder:

- another room;
- quiet porch or yard;
- quiet friend's house;
- empty area of a park;
- parked car in safe conditions;
- a quiet public edge at an off-peak time, only if the dog is ready.

Every new location is a partial reset. Lower expectations and leave while the dog can still succeed.

## Do not turn the mat into restraint

A settle mat should not be used to force a worried dog to remain near something they are trying to escape.

If the dog repeatedly tries to leave, ask why. The environment may be too difficult.

For visitors, success may mean the dog remains behind a gate on the mat for the whole visit. Greeting is not required.

## Use a simple readiness check

Before increasing difficulty, ask whether the dog can:

- move to the mat on one cue in the current location;
- remain comfortably for the current duration;
- take food normally if food is being used;
- look away from you;
- respond to the release cue;
- recover quickly after a small distraction.

If not, strengthen the current level.

If lying down, movement, handling, or settling has suddenly become difficult, consider physical comfort and veterinary advice rather than assuming the dog needs more repetitions.
