A storm can change more than pool chemistry.

Heavy rain, wind, debris, power interruption, flooding, unusual water level, and physical damage can affect several systems at once.

After significant weather, use this order:

**SAFETY → PHYSICAL DAMAGE → CIRCULATION → DEBRIS → TESTING → TREATMENT → VERIFICATION**

## 1. Safety comes before restart

Before approaching or restarting equipment, look for:

- flooded or submerged electrical equipment;
- damaged wiring or enclosures;
- fallen objects;
- cracked or displaced plumbing;
- damaged filter or pressure-containing equipment;
- gas or heater concerns;
- structural damage;
- major leaks.

If electrical equipment has been flooded, physically damaged, or exposed to an unsafe environment, do not simply switch it back on.

Repeatedly resetting a tripped protective device is not troubleshooting.

Use appropriately qualified help when the hazard is outside normal owner maintenance.

## 2. Record physical damage before changing the system

Photograph obvious damage where it is safe to do so.

Check:

- water level;
- equipment pad;
- visible pipes and fittings;
- cover position;
- filter gauge;
- pump condition;
- skimmer and baskets;
- large debris.

The objective is to understand what changed before the evidence is removed.

## 3. Re-establish understandable circulation

Once the equipment is safe to operate, confirm:

- the pump primes;
- return flow is present;
- filter pressure behaves plausibly;
- persistent air is not entering the system;
- no new leak appears;
- pump settings and valve positions are understood.

If circulation is abnormal, diagnose that before relying on filtration or making a broad chemical correction.

A cloudy pool with low pressure and air requires a different response from cloudy water with high pressure and weak flow.

Use the [filter-pressure guide](/pool-troubleshooting/pool-filter-pressure-high-or-low/) and [air-bubbles guide](/pool-troubleshooting/air-bubbles-at-pool-returns/) when the equipment clues point in those directions.

## 4. Remove debris

Remove leaves, branches, and other physical contamination using safe methods appropriate to the pool.

Storm debris increases the load on the circulation and filtration system.

As cleanup proceeds, watch pressure and flow against the filter's normal baseline and model-specific service criterion.

## 5. Obtain current water tests

Do not rely on measurements taken before the storm.

Once circulation is understood and the water has been mixed according to the test instructions, obtain a current baseline relevant to the decisions you need to make.

Verify surprising results before a large correction.

Useful starting measurements often include free chlorine and pH, with other tests brought in when they answer a specific diagnostic question.

## 6. Treat the diagnosed condition

A post-storm pool may become green, cloudy, low in sanitizer, or affected in several ways at once.

Reconnect the symptoms instead of treating each one independently.

For example:

- green + cloudy + unexpectedly low chlorine can point toward increased sanitizer demand and biological growth;
- cloudy + high pressure + weaker flow can make filtration an immediate limiting factor;
- low pressure + air + low water level shifts the first priority toward water supply and circulation.

Use the [green-water guide](/pool-troubleshooting/green-pool-algae-or-metals/) or [chlorine-loss guide](/pool-troubleshooting/chlorine-keeps-disappearing/) when those symptoms dominate.

Any product use should follow the current label for the exact product and the known pool volume.

Do not improvise chemical combinations.

## 7. Verify stability, not just appearance

Recovery is not complete because the water looks better for an hour.

Confirm that:

- circulation remains normal;
- pressure is understandable;
- sanitizer can be maintained;
- pH is credible;
- clarity is recovering;
- no equipment warning or leak appears;
- the problem does not immediately recur.

## Do not automatically drain the pool

A high water level after a storm can tempt owners to remove a large amount of water.

Major water-level changes require understanding the pool, site conditions, and manufacturer or professional guidance.

Heavily draining or emptying a pool can create its own risks.

The post-storm objective is to rebuild a safe, known operating baseline before making large interventions.
