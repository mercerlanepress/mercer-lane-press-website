**A floor that feels cool does not necessarily mean your underfloor heating has stopped working.** The more useful starting question is whether the **room air temperature** reaches the temperature you actually need, when you need it.

## First, identify which temperature you mean

- **Room air temperature** describes the occupied space. Use the thermostat's room reading, if correctly identified, or a separate thermometer in a consistent place.
- **Floor-surface temperature** is the finished surface beneath your feet. A comfortable room can have a surface that feels neutral.
- **Water flow temperature** applies to wet underfloor systems and is not the same as a safe floor-surface temperature.

A thermostat may display a target, measured air temperature or floor-probe value. Consult its exact user manual before reading a single number as proof of a fault.

## A safe fifteen-minute first assessment

1. **Check for hazards first.** If there is a material leak near electrics, a repeatedly tripping circuit, dangerous overheating, exposed wiring, a gas concern or a carbon monoxide alarm, stop the routine checks and follow the appropriate emergency or professional route.
2. **Identify the type of floor heating.** Wet systems circulate warm water; electric mats or cables have different controls and no water manifold for that zone. A home may have both.
3. **Record the room and its target.** Note actual air temperature, desired air temperature, clock time and whether the shortfall is mild or substantial.
4. **Check ordinary operating mode.** Compare scheduled, holiday, away, off, manual hold and temporary override states using the actual model guide.
5. **Read any heating symbol correctly.** A flame symbol can mean a request for heat rather than confirmation that a pump, valve or individual loop is circulating.
6. **Look at other rooms.** Is the issue local to one zone, shared among rooms, or whole-house? Note any owner-visible fault code on the heat source.

## What a slow floor can mean

A deeply embedded wet system can store heat in a screed or slab. A sudden change of target may take much longer to influence room comfort than a radiator. A lightweight overlay can behave differently. You cannot reliably diagnose a fault by measuring only a few minutes of response, and increasing the target to an extreme value does not guarantee faster heat delivery.

If the room has recently been unheated, refer to the manufacturer's documented expected response. Do not repeatedly change controls while trying to establish a baseline.

## Wet and electric floors are not checked in the same way

For a **wet system**, note the heat source (boiler, heat pump or other), the room thermostat and any accessible existing zone labels. Do **not** touch manifold flow meters, balancing valves, pumps or mixing settings to test a theory.

For an **electric system**, check the thermostat's normal user mode and any displayed floor-probe warning. A recurring protective trip, damaged wiring or floor-sensor error needs an appropriately competent electrician or system specialist; do not bypass sensors or open a back box.

## When should you call an engineer?

If a room repeatedly fails to reach its target over a meaningful period, or a warning persists, give the specialist a short factual record: room, air temperature, target, time, thermostat model and mode, other rooms, weather and recent changes. If urgent hazards are present, do not wait for a diary.
