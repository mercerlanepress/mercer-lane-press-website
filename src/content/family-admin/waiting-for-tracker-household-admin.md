Many household tasks disappear at exactly the point when your own immediate action is finished.

You send the school an email. A contractor says a quote will arrive. A provider promises a refund. An office says it will call next week. A replacement document has been requested.

Your task list may now look complete, but the household is still depending on somebody else.

That state needs a home.

## Record the dependency, not just the original task

A useful Waiting For entry answers six questions:

1. **What are you waiting for?** Name the reply, refund, quote, document, confirmation, decision, or other outcome.
2. **Who owes the next move?** Record the person or organization.
3. **When did you ask?** Keep the request date.
4. **Was a timescale promised?** Record the stated or expected response window when one exists.
5. **When will you review or chase?** Give the item a visible follow-up date.
6. **Where is the evidence?** Note where the sent message, confirmation, reference number, or other useful record can be found.

A status field can then show whether the item is open, received, resolved, promised for a new date, escalated through an appropriate route, deferred, or closed.

## Choose chase dates from the real situation

The aim is not to create constant checking.

If an organization gives a response window, use it. If no timescale is provided, choose a review date based on the consequence of delay.

A low-consequence enquiry can wait longer than an item blocking travel, access, a school obligation, a repair, or another time-sensitive step.

This makes chasing systematic rather than emotional.

## Keep your action separate from the provider’s promise

Suppose an insurer asks you to send a document and then says it will issue an updated confirmation.

Those are two different states:

- **your action:** send the requested document;
- **Waiting For:** updated confirmation from the insurer.

When the document is sent, your task can close. The dependency remains open until the promised confirmation arrives or the household decides on another appropriate next step.

This separation makes it much easier to see who owes what next.

## Use one chronology for repeated follow-ups

A common failure is creating a new task every time you chase.

That produces several versions of the same unresolved issue and makes it harder to reconstruct what happened.

Instead, keep one record and roll the status forward:

- request sent;
- first promised date;
- first chase;
- revised promise;
- second contact or official alternative route if appropriate;
- resolution or deliberate closure.

For a longer provider problem, keep a concise factual trail with dates, contact route, reference number where available, what was requested or agreed, what is supposed to happen next, and where the supporting evidence is stored.

If the matter becomes a formal complaint, appeal, legal issue, or another high-stakes process, use current official or qualified guidance for the substantive route. The household tracker is there to preserve dates, actions, and evidence—not to invent rights or deadlines.

## Review Waiting For every week

A Waiting For list works because it is part of the weekly review.

During the [20-minute Weekly Admin Reset](/family-admin/20-minute-weekly-family-admin-reset/):

- scan the items whose chase dates have arrived;
- check whether a response has already been received;
- update the next date after any contact;
- link blocked household tasks to the dependency where useful;
- close items that are genuinely resolved.

Items that are not due for attention can remain quiet.

## Worked example: a promised refund

A provider confirms that a refund will be issued.

Record:

- provider;
- date of the promise;
- reference number if useful;
- the promised timescale;
- the date you will check;
- the location of the confirmation email.

If the date passes, use the provider’s current contact process and add the new contact to the same record. If a new timescale is given, move the review date.

The item closes only after the expected outcome has actually arrived or the household has deliberately decided on another route.

That small distinction—**sent is not finished**—closes one of the biggest gaps in ordinary household admin.
